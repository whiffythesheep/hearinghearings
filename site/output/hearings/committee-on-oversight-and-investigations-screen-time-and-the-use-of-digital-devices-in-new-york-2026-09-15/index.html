Screen Time and the Use of Digital Devices in New York City Public Schools

Committee on Oversight and Investigations | Committee on Education

Chairs: Shekar Krishnan, Eric Dinowitz
September 15, 2026 · 4hrs 34m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The joint Committee on Oversight and Investigations and Committee on Education held an oversight hearing on September 15, 2026, examining screen time and digital device use in New York City Public Schools. The hearing was prompted by mounting concern from parents, educators and advocates about the proliferation of screens in classrooms since the COVID-19 pandemic and the DOE's new AI and screen time policy announced on September 2, 2026, just thirteen days before the hearing and four school days into the new academic year.

Opening statements from Chair Krishnan, Chair Dinowitz, Speaker Julie Menin and Public Advocate Jumaane Williams established a broadly shared framework: the pandemic-era pivot to screens was understandable but became an entrenched default rather than a temporary emergency measure; ed tech companies have exploited this dependency for profit while exposing children to addictive design, data harvesting and unproven instructional tools; and the new DOE policy, while a meaningful step, leaves too many questions unanswered and too many loopholes open. All four speakers called for stronger mandates, more rigorous evidence standards for vendors and greater parental awareness. Menin noted that reading proficiency for third graders dropped nearly 14% compared to the prior year and flagged that over half of American teenagers report using AI for schoolwork, with one in ten saying AI does most of it for them. Williams called attention to his office's new report on responsible AI use and raised specific concerns about digital bathroom passes and voice recognition tools for young children as examples of poorly scrutinised procurement.

A panel of advocates and parents testified before the DOE, setting a critical tone. Neuroscientist and parent Kalidas Saleh linked excessive ed tech to increased diagnoses of ADHD, autism and mental health conditions and called for a full moratorium and audit of all ed tech platforms. Sarah Sayeed of MASA described how screen-heavy instruction had widened inequities for immigrant families, including a situation where her son spent three months without headphones for screen-based instruction and no teacher noticed. David Monahan of Fair Play cited a California court verdict establishing that Google intentionally designed YouTube with addictive mechanics and pressed the Council to mandate rather than recommend limits. Navid Hassan of the Panel for Educational Policy argued for building sovereign municipal tech infrastructure and reducing dependence on vendors whose products sometimes cannot comply with student data privacy laws.

First Deputy Chancellor Daniel Johnson led the DOE testimony, accompanied by Deputy CIO Scott Strickland, privacy and policy staff, and officials from the offices of multilingual learners, special education and computer science. He outlined the new policy: no individual screens for grades K through 2; a recommended maximum of 30 minutes daily for grades 3 through 5; 45 minutes for grades 6 through 8; device use in high school governed by instructional purpose; a full moratorium on student-facing generative AI through grade 8; and three narrow permitted uses of AI in high school involving five pilots, career technical education programs and a mandatory AI literacy course. Key exemptions include assistive technology for students with disabilities and English language learners, digital assessments and diagnostics, remote instruction days and centrally approved programs such as robotics and coding. Under questioning, Strickland confirmed that as of the Wednesday before the hearing, YouTube had been blocked on all student devices, including at home, via Google Admin Console and device-level filtering agents.

The hearing's most substantive exchanges probed the policy's weaknesses. Council members pressed hard on the distinction between recommendations and mandates, with Johnson acknowledging the limits are non-negotiable in practice but defending the softer language as necessary to avoid a punitive first-year implementation. The digital assessment exemption drew sustained criticism: Chair Morano pointed out that a child could spend 30 minutes on instructional screens and then an additional hour on digital assessments with no policy constraint, since the child's brain does not know the difference. Chair Dinowitz pushed for the state to return to handwritten assessments. On data privacy, Chief Privacy Officer Dennis Doyle confirmed the DOE relies on the school officials exception under FERPA rather than obtaining affirmative parental consent for sharing student data with third-party vendors. He was unable to confirm how many data privacy audits had been conducted in the prior school year. On vendor accountability, officials acknowledged that independent third-party efficacy research is not yet a standard requirement for ed tech procurement, and that the framework for the five high school AI pilots had not yet been finalised, pilot schools had not been selected and modules had not been rolled out, even though the school year had already begun. The DOE also confirmed it spent $105 million on educational software in fiscal year 2026 and is still working through which of the 73 technology contracts in the fiscal year 2027 budget deal remain appropriate under the new policy.

▸ Numbers
  • At least six states, including Alabama, Iowa, Maine, Tennessee, Virginia and Utah, have enacted laws restricting classroom screen time; at least 10 other states have enacted similar legislation.
  • Los Angeles Unified School District caps screen time at six hours per week for high school students, including homework.
  • More than half of American teenagers report using AI tools for schoolwork; one in ten say AI does all or most of their schoolwork.
  • Third through eighth grade overall reading scores dropped six percentage points in the most recent year compared to the prior year.
  • Third grade reading proficiency dropped nearly 14% compared to the prior year.
  • The DOE policy bans individual screen use entirely for grades K through 2.
  • The DOE policy recommends a maximum of 30 minutes of individual screen use per day for grades 3 through 5.
  • The DOE policy recommends a maximum of 45 minutes of individual screen use per day for grades 6 through 8.
  • The generative AI moratorium covers students in grades K through 8, affecting approximately 600,000 students.
  • High school AI literacy short-form course is 45 minutes in length.
  • High school AI literacy long-form course for students in AI pilots or career technical education pathways is 12 to 15 hours in length.
  • The DOE is running five AI pilot programs in high schools, using three categories of tools: a writing supplement, a math tool and a project-based learning tool, plus a tool for teacher-designed customised solutions.
  • Pilot AI tool sessions are intended to be low-dose, approximately 15 minutes of student interaction at a time.
  • The DOE is conducting three universal screener assessments per year, administered roughly in October, January and May.
  • Digital assessments and diagnostics are fully exempt from the 30 and 45 minute screen time limits.
  • As of Wednesday before the hearing, YouTube was blocked on all student devices, including at home, via device-level filtering.
  • The DOE spent $105 million on educational software in fiscal year 2026, covering both school-based and central spending.
  • There are 73 technology contracts in the fiscal year 2027 budget deal.
  • The DOE announced it had disabled AI components in 38 previously approved programs, though the full list had not been published as of the hearing date.
  • The DOE has not established an affirmative parental consent process for sharing student data with third-party vendors, relying instead on the school officials exception under FERPA.
▸ Action Points
  • DOE to provide the committees with the full list of all 38 programs whose AI components were disabled or discontinued, specifying which are being discontinued entirely and on what basis.
  • DOE to share with the committees and the public the full list of research citations used to develop the 30-minute and 45-minute screen time recommendations, including sources beyond the Brookings Institution and American Academy of Pediatrics materials already published on the DOE website.
  • DOE to confirm the number of data privacy audits conducted in the 2025-2026 school year and provide that information to the committees as soon as possible, ideally during the hearing day.
  • DOE Chief Privacy Officer to review whether the FERPA school officials exception should be replaced or supplemented with an affirmative parental notification or consent policy for sharing student data with third-party vendors, in light of evolving AI and data practices.
  • DOE to issue guidance to schools clarifying that the digital assessment exemption should not be used to expand effective screen time beyond the policy's intended limits, and that assessment days should be balanced by reducing or eliminating other screen use.
  • DOE to publish the full framework, rubric, evaluation metrics and data collection plan for the five high school AI pilots before the pilots launch.
  • DOE to apply to and receive pilot applications from high schools, evaluate them for geographic and demographic representation, and launch pilots in the fall of 2026.
  • DOE to roll out an application process to all high schools for participation in the AI pilot program.
  • DOE to provide committees with a list indicating which of the hundreds of approved software products contain AI components and their current compliance status under the new policy.
  • DOE to work with vendors to confirm whether AI components in existing products can be disabled and to update schools on product-by-product status.
  • DOE to report back to committees on which of the 73 technology contracts in the fiscal year 2027 budget deal required amendment or discontinuation due to the new screen time and AI policy.
  • DOE to provide the projected cost of implementing the AI and screen time policy in fiscal year 2027, including staffing for expanded contract review, the Technology in Schools Coalition and AI literacy modules.
  • DOE to clarify and publish a clear list of which programs and uses are and are not exempt from the screen time regulations, to address school-level confusion in the first weeks of implementation.
  • DOE to work with the Division of Early Childhood Education to address reports of teachers using personal cell phones to show movies to 3-K children during nap time.
  • DOE Technology in Schools Coalition to meet monthly, produce a public end-of-year report evaluating policy implementation, and consider whether the coalition should continue beyond year one.
  • DOE to explore with the UFT and relevant stakeholders whether paper-and-pencil testing options can be made available for students and parents who wish to opt out of digital assessments.
  • DOE to consider and report back on whether K through 2 students should be required to leave Chromebooks at school rather than bringing them home.
  • DOE to develop and share with the committees a vendor accountability standard requiring independent third-party efficacy evidence before ed tech products are approved for classroom use.
  • DOE to explore whether gamified apps not currently captured by the AI moratorium should be separately restricted or banned from school use.
  • DOE to confirm whether it will sign on to the Microsoft agreement referenced during CM Narcisse's questioning, following consultation with Vice President Vaccaro.
