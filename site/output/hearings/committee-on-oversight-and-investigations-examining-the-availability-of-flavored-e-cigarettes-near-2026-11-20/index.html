Examining the Availability of Flavored E-Cigarettes Near New York City Schools

Committee on Oversight and Investigations | Committee on Consumer and Worker Protection

Chairs: Shekar Krishnan, Harvey D. Epstein
November 20, 2026 · 1hr 31m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The joint hearing of the Committee on Oversight and Investigations and the Committee on Consumer and Worker Protection, chaired by CM Shekar Krishnan and CM Harvey Epstein, examined two related matters: the findings of the Council's Oversight and Investigations Division report on the availability of illegal flavored e-cigarettes near New York City schools, and Int 1167, a bill that would grant the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection (DCWP) authority to seal the premises of e-cigarette retailers found to be repeatedly selling prohibited flavored vapes or making underage sales. The report's core finding was damning: 40% of businesses surveyed near schools offered prohibited flavored e-cigarettes, and 100% of unlicensed retailers were selling illegal flavored vapes despite having no legal authorization to sell any nicotine or tobacco products at all. New York State banned flavored nicotine vapor products in 2020 after finding that a quarter of NYC high school students used vapes, particularly fruit- and candy-flavored varieties.

DCWP's Chief of Staff Carlos Ortiz and Deputy General Counsel Andrew Schrank testified that the agency is working hard within its current authority: 12,000 tobacco and roughly 10,300 e-cigarette retailer inspections conducted in 2026, nearly 4,000 summonses issued, $3.6 million in penalties collected, and 240 recidivist tobacco retailers sealed since 2023. They expressed strong support for Int 1167, noting that their existing sealing authority covers tobacco retailers and has proven effective, generating $3.8 million in collected penalties since 2023 precisely because businesses must pay outstanding fines to get the padlock removed. Extending equivalent authority to e-cigarette retailers, they argued, would be a significant enforcement upgrade. DCWP also raised a separate priority: requiring disclosure of LLC ownership to city agencies, noting that bad actors routinely evade enforcement and fine collection by dissolving one LLC and opening a new one at the same location under the same beneficial ownership.

The hearing exposed serious structural gaps in the city's enforcement apparatus. The penalty collection rate against unlicensed e-cigarette retailers stood at a staggering 0.5%, meaning over $1.1 million in assessed fines has gone essentially uncollected. Council members pressed hard on why: DCWP acknowledged that fly-by-night operators never intend to pay, dissolve their entities when enforcement pressure rises, and re-open under new LLCs. The process for sealing even a blatantly illegal retailer requires multiple inspections, multiple OATH adjudications, and a formal notice-of-opportunity process, meaning a school-adjacent illegal vape shop can remain open for a year or more before any closure action is possible. DCWP confirmed it has no emergency sealing power, even in egregious cases. Members also surfaced the problem of "clear vapes," products marketed as unflavored but which often contain detectable flavors, used by retailers as a legal grey area. DCWP said it is working with the Department of Health on guidance and product testing to close this loophole. The hearing also revealed that the city has essentially no visibility into or authority over vape wholesalers and distributors, meaning the supply chain delivering illegal products to hundreds of retail locations is largely untouched. DCWP could not name the responsible city agency for wholesaler oversight.

A single public witness, David Schwartz of D Distributors, representing licensed wholesalers and retailers, was notably pointed: he named several companies already the subject of city and federal lawsuits as illegal suppliers, expressed surprise that DCWP was apparently unaware of these cases, and argued that the illegal market is destroying law-abiding licensed businesses. He called for a coordinated enforcement community involving licensed wholesalers as intelligence sources, noted that the city had declined from 10,000 cigarette retail licenses to under 5,000 as illegal operators crowded out legal ones, and supported Int 1167. He offered to share intelligence on known illegal distribution locations with the Council. By the close of the hearing, the committee had laid out a multi-layered agenda: pass Int 1167 to extend sealing authority to e-cigarette retailers, pursue LLC transparency to cut off the entity-flipping evasion tactic, engage state partners on data-sharing for LLC ownership records, investigate the wholesale and distribution supply chain, consider faster enforcement triggers near schools, and pursue more aggressive fine collection as a revenue source for the agency.

▸ Numbers
  • 25% of New York City high school students used vapes at the time New York State banned flavored nicotine vapor products in 2020, particularly fruit- and candy-flavored varieties.
  • 40% of businesses surveyed near NYC schools by the Council's Oversight and Investigations Division offered prohibited flavored e-cigarettes.
  • 100% of unlicensed retailers visited by investigators were found to be selling illegal flavored vapes despite holding no license to sell any tobacco or nicotine products.
  • DCWP conducted approximately 12,000 tobacco retail dealer inspections in 2026 to date.
  • DCWP conducted approximately 10,300 e-cigarette retail dealer inspections in 2026 to date.
  • DCWP issued nearly 4,000 summonses in 2026 to date.
  • DCWP collected $3.6 million in penalties in 2026 to date.
  • DCWP sealed 240 recidivist tobacco retailers since 2023.
  • Sealing authority applied to tobacco retailers has generated $3.8 million in collected penalties since 2023, as businesses must pay outstanding fines to have padlocks removed.
  • Tobacco program inspections decreased 32% from fiscal year 2025 to fiscal year 2026.
  • Unlicensed e-cigarette retailers have accrued approximately $1.112 million in assessed penalties, of which only 0.5% has been collected.
  • DCWP collected over $230,000 from businesses targeted for tobacco sealings in the current fiscal year alone.
  • DCWP inspects every licensed e-cigarette retailer at least once per year.
  • Retailers with underage sale violations receive at least three follow-up inspections per year.
  • Unlicensed retailers found in violation can receive up to four inspections per year, potentially generating hundreds of thousands of dollars in penalties per location per year.
  • A minimum of two to three adjudicated OATH summonses are required before DCWP can initiate a sealing action against an e-cigarette retailer, even for unlicensed operators.
  • The city of New York once had 10,000 cigarette retail licenses; that number has fallen to approximately 4,990 today.
  • New York City once had 40 cigarette tax agents operating within the city; that number has fallen to zero.
  • Approximately 40 vape products have passed the FDA's PMTA process and are legally authorized for sale nationally.
  • 99% of discarded vapor products recovered in field surveys were flavored, according to testimony from the licensed wholesaler representative.
▸ Action Points
  • DCWP to reprioritize inspection efforts to focus on retailers located near schools, using data from the Council's Oversight and Investigations Division report to target specific locations.
  • DCWP to work with the Council to advance Int 1167 to extend sealing authority to e-cigarette retailers, aligning e-cigarette enforcement powers with existing tobacco sealing authority.
  • DCWP to investigate whether the Council's recently passed LLC transparency law, or an interagency data-sharing agreement with the New York State Department of State, can be used to obtain beneficial ownership information for LLCs operating illegal vape retail businesses.
  • DCWP to report back to the committee on the specific barriers to obtaining LLC ownership data from the state on a timely basis.
  • DCWP to work with the Law Department and city partners to identify which agency has regulatory authority over vape and e-cigarette wholesalers and distributors, and report back to the committee.
  • DCWP to investigate the supply chain for illegal flavored vapes, including tracing common brands found in violations back to their distributors, and share findings with the committee.
  • DCWP to share any wholesale or distribution intelligence it gathers with relevant city and state agencies, including potential referrals to the Law Department's Affirmative Litigation Division.
  • DCWP to develop and issue particularized guidance to inspectors on how to identify and classify "clear vapes" that contain flavors as prohibited products.
  • DCWP to work with the Department of Health to complete product testing on clear vapes to support summons issuance and inspector training.
  • DCWP to explore a proposed prohibition on vape and tobacco vending machines in the city, in collaboration with the Department of Health and Mental Hygiene.
  • DCWP to look into ways to increase fine collection rates from unlicensed e-cigarette retailers, potentially through more aggressive referrals to the Law Department and third-party debt collection.
  • DCWP to compile and provide to the committee data on how many fines have been issued to landlords for leasing to illegal vape retailers since 2023.
  • DCWP to consider whether the Council should incorporate emergency or expedited sealing authority for school-adjacent illegal vape retailers into Int 1167 or related legislation.
  • Council's Oversight and Investigations Committee to follow up with D Distributors (David Schwartz) to receive intelligence on known illegal wholesale and distribution operations for illegal vapes and cigarettes.
  • CM Krishnan to coordinate with committee staff to establish a channel for licensed wholesalers and distributors to share intelligence on illegal operators with the Council and relevant agencies.
  • DCWP to evaluate whether advertising and product display violations by licensed retailers near schools can be more systematically enforced, and to encourage community members to submit complaints identifying the proximate school to assist with measurement and inspection targeting.
