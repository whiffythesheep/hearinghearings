Interagency Coordination for Older Adults

Committee on Aging

Chair: Susan Zhuang
June 16, 2026 · 2hrs 17m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Aging held an oversight hearing on interagency coordination for older adults, alongside consideration of Int 0230-2026, a bill that would create a financial assistance program for in-home preventative adaptations for older adults and people with disabilities, and Res 0373-2026, calling on Albany to pass long-term care benefits legislation. The hearing was the committee's main vehicle for examining the Cabinet for Older New Yorkers, the 30-agency interagency body codified by Local Law 64 of 2024, which NYC Aging Commissioner Dr. Lisa Scott-McKenzie described as a budget-neutral mechanism for systemic, long-term change rather than a constituent services operation. The commissioner's testimony was heavy on structure and initiative names, lighter on hard outcomes and metrics, and the new commissioner's repeated references to not yet having chaired a cabinet meeting left a notable credibility gap when Council members pressed for specifics.

On Int 0230-2026, the commissioner offered qualified support, endorsing the intent while flagging unresolved questions about operationalization. Council Member Hudson, who introduced the bill, made the case on affordability grounds: the median income for a senior living alone is $22,900, one-third of older adults have a functional disability, and falls and mobility barriers are primary drivers of institutionalization. Public testimony from the Legal Aid Society's Director of Elderly Practice broadly supported the bill but raised substantive implementation concerns, including the risk of eviction for tenants who modify rent-stabilized apartments without landlord approval, the absence of a fair hearing right for denied applicants, and the difficulty older adults face proving disability eligibility once past 67 and no longer covered by Social Security Disability rather than retirement benefits. These are real design problems that will need to be resolved before the bill can function as intended.

The hearing surfaced persistent, structural problems with interagency coordination that the cabinet has not visibly addressed. Council Member Zhuang pressed on the one-stop-shop question, noting that seniors routinely end up in her district office because they cannot navigate which agency handles which issue. The commissioner's answer, pointing to the Aging Connect phone line and its warm handoff model, was met skeptically given testimony from United Neighborhood Houses and LiveOn NY that providers cannot identify the NYCHA-DFTA liaison, tickets for facility repairs get closed without resolution, and center directors have been personally threatened with arrest due to safety violations that neither NYCHA nor DFTA would take responsibility for fixing. Council Member Brewer pushed on whether the cabinet was duplicating rather than building on two decades of Age Friendly New York work, and on Mitchell-Lama housing, flagging that Local Law 44 of 2024 requiring HPD to send pre-filled SCRIE applications to Mitchell-Lama residents may not be being complied with. The commissioner committed to adding this to the June 25 cabinet agenda. Council Member Zhuang also highlighted the LGBTQ plus older adult commission required under Local Law 170 of 2023, which has been seated only partially and has produced no output more than two years after passage, drawing pointed frustration. The commissioner, three months into the role, could offer little beyond a commitment to do better.

External testimony reinforced the pattern of coordination failure. LiveOn NY called for opening cabinet meetings to the public, expanding membership to include providers and academics, and having the cabinet issue large-scale recommendations to HPD and HRA rather than confining itself to internal interagency logistics. A Bronx resident presented a data tracker of chronic elevator offender buildings, noting that 33 single-elevator buildings in New York City sit within a quarter mile of DFTA-funded sites and that the cabinet has no data bridge to connect elevator failure patterns to aging services outreach. A tenant advocate testified against NYCHA's PACT privatization program, arguing it threatens the aging-in-place infrastructure of public housing communities. On food security and elder abuse, Council Member Zhuang described a constituent case of overnight SNAP fraud that NYPD refused to take a report on, and a separate sexual abuse case involving a non-English-speaking senior that was similarly mishandled until her office intervened, illustrating the gap between the commissioner's description of coordination mechanisms and what actually happens at street level.
