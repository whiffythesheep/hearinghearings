Secure Detention Conditions and Youth Incarceration Policies in New York City

Committee on Children and Youth

Chair: Althea V. Stevens
October 27, 2026 · 4hrs 28m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Children and Youth held an oversight hearing on secure detention conditions and youth incarceration policies in New York City, chaired by CM Althea Stevens. The hearing also considered four pieces of legislation: Int 0447-2026 (requiring a guide to alternatives to incarceration for court stakeholders), Int 0629-2026 (requiring population review teams for youth in secure detention), Int 0977-2026 (requiring annual staff training on identifying sexually exploited youth), and Res 0274-2026 (calling on the state to include New York City in Raise the Age program funding). The hearing drew an expanded committee presence, with CMs Felder, Joseph, Williams, Nurse, Hanks, and Restler all attending, reflecting the political weight the issue has accumulated.

The core crisis: Horizon and Crossroads juvenile detention facilities have a combined certified capacity of 244 beds but held 352 young people as of the week of the hearing. Over 96 percent of those held are Black and Hispanic youth. The vast majority are pretrial detainees, some held for over four years. Many are sleeping in classrooms, chapels, and halls. The population has more than doubled since the start of the Adams administration, rising from roughly 140 when Adams took office. Chair Stevens had previously called publicly for the release of 150 young people, arguing — with some success — that coordinated action by courts, prosecutors, and defenders had demonstrated this was achievable. The hearing's underlying tension was between an administration offering a plan and a committee demanding urgency that looked more like a crisis response than a managed process.

Bronx DA Darcel Clark testified as the first witness, speaking informally on behalf of all five borough DAs. Her testimony was notably substantive. She described the Bronx's disproportionate share of youth gun violence and the structural information-sharing failures that hamper her office's ability to manage cases. Her key practical complaint: unlike Rikers, her office does not receive daily detention population lists from ACS for youth held at Horizon and Crossroads, making it impossible to proactively identify candidates for early release or case prioritization. She testified that the law restricts her access to social, educational, and mental health information about young people, which she argued she needs not to punish youth but to make appropriate disposition decisions. She described her newly established Youth Justice Bureau, three pilot intervention programs for the Bronx, and the recently funded Bronx Restore prevention program. When asked whether more arrests correlated with safer communities, she flatly rejected the premise, noting that New York City had 2,300 murders in 1990 and fewer than 200 now, while arguing that resourced communities — not incarceration rates — drive safety.

The administration panel was led by the ACS Commissioner and supported by colleagues from the Mayor's Office of Criminal Justice and the Department of Probation. Their testimony outlined a September 2026 plan including expanded community-based alternatives, new construction at both facilities, additional hiring, and a new Intensive Community Monitoring Connect program for 150 youth. Chair Stevens and several colleagues were visibly frustrated by what they characterized as a plan that lacked urgency and was rolling out in November when the crisis had been building for years. CM Stevens pressed the panel directly: a COVID-era model for rapid population review involving prosecutors, defenders, and courts had worked before — why was it not being deployed now? The administration's answers were largely procedural, referencing working groups, data-sharing agreements in progress, and forthcoming program launches. CM Stevens concluded by dismissing the panel with a pointed statement that every adult in the room should consider themselves to be failing these young people.

Several sharp exchanges occurred during the administration panel. CM Stevens raised real-time reports from staff, received during the hearing itself, that classes were being canceled and young people were sleeping on floors and in chairs. She also raised photographs shared with her of a water gun fight organized at one of the facilities, involving young people charged with gun offenses — a detail that provoked genuine anger. The administration confirmed it did not yet have a formal mechanism for facility staff to flag cases for early release consideration, that there are essentially no programs for young people whose primary need is mental health (the administration eventually conceded this gap explicitly after several evasive exchanges), and that data-sharing agreements with state oversight bodies needed to close information silos are still being negotiated. The administration did provide some concrete detail: new community-based program contracts are being backdated to October 1, ICM Connect is launching November 16 with Bronx pilot capacity for 150 youth, and the Horizon annex is now projected to complete by end of 2027 rather than 2029.

▸ Numbers
  • As of October 23, 2026, 352 young people were held in secure detention at Horizon and Crossroads combined (174 at Horizon, 178 at Crossroads), against a certified capacity of 244 beds.
  • When Mayor Adams took office, the secure detention population was approximately 140; it has since risen to approximately 360, more than doubling.
  • 96 percent of young people held in juvenile offender and adolescent offender cases are Black and Hispanic.
  • 346 of the 352 young people in detention have cases in the Youth Part of Supreme Court; only 6 have cases solely in Family Court.
  • The median age of youth in secure detention is 17; 26 of the 352 are age 15 or younger; 8 are girls.
  • Some youth held as adolescent offenders have been in pretrial detention for more than six months; some have been held for more than four years.
  • 109 young people currently do not have bedrooms; 74 of those are on halls, with the remainder in classrooms, chapels, or other repurposed spaces.
  • Horizon has 123 bedrooms and 40 bathrooms; Crossroads has 122 bedrooms and 36 bathrooms.
  • Since July 2025, 20 youth bathrooms have been renovated and 1 staff bathroom converted to youth use.
  • The Horizon annex under construction will add 56 bedrooms and 8 classrooms, with completion now projected for end of 2027 rather than 2029; auxiliary housing at Crossroads will add 28 bedrooms and 2 classrooms.
  • Temporary classroom trailers at Crossroads will accommodate 32 students (16 per trailer) and are awaiting final state approval.
  • Since the start of the Mamdani administration, ACS has hired 166 youth development specialists and 52 special officers for detention.
  • 85 emergency response staff (special officers and supervisors) are currently being recruited; hiring is in early stages.
  • 71 young people in detention are currently registered to vote.
  • ACS invested an additional $12.2 million in community-based alternatives starting in FY2026, growing in FY2027.
  • The Families Rising functional family therapy program budget is being increased by $344,000, from approximately $1.6 million to $2 million.
  • The EXALT alternatives program is receiving an additional $412,000 per year; the Fortune Society received approximately $800,000 in additional funding; the Center for Community Alternatives received $618,000 in additional funding; all backdated to October 1.
  • Non-secure detention currently has 87 beds across 7 sites with 50 youth placed; 9 additional beds are being brought online.
  • ICM Connect will launch November 16 with capacity for 150 young people, with caseloads of approximately 10 per worker.
  • 39 young people in detention are currently enrolled in college classes through Hostos Community College and BMCC; 316 have taken college classes over 7 semesters; 163 have graduated since 2020.
  • 367 young people received workforce certifications in detention last year; the facility is on track for over 400 this year.
  • The Bronx accounts for roughly one third of youth gun arrests, one third of youth shooting victims, and nearly half of youth shooting defendants citywide.
  • Bronx DA's Youth Justice Bureau has 9 ADAs, 3 leadership staff, and approximately 8 professional support staff, totaling around 20 people.
  • Bronx Restore prevention program received $3 million from the Governor and is targeting 30 teens and young adults; participants will be paid $25 per hour.
  • The rate of youth-on-staff assaults with injuries in detention fell from 0.21 to 0.13 per hundred youth in the average daily population per the FY2026 Mayor's Management Report.
  • Five former Horizon youth development specialists pleaded guilty on October 24, 2026 to accepting over $50,000 in cash bribes for smuggling contraband since 2024.
  • State funding of $300 million in Raise the Age funds is in the city budget baseline; the FY2026 spending plan has been submitted to the state for approval; the FY2027 plan is still being developed.
