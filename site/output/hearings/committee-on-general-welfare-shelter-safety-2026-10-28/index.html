Shelter Safety

Committee on General Welfare

Chair: Crystal Hudson
October 28, 2026 · 3hrs 50m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on General Welfare held an oversight hearing on shelter safety, alongside consideration of two bills. Int 0099-2026, sponsored by Council Member Gale Brewer, would require the Department of Homeless Services to report annually on the number and percentage of families with children placed in shelters in the same or adjoining zip code as their prior address. Int 0387-2026, sponsored by the Public Advocate, would require annual reporting on critical incidents in shelters, including assaults, sexual assaults, domestic violence, weapons possession, arson and theft, along with information on security upgrades and NYPD agreements.

Three shelter residents testified first, all members of the Coalition for the Homeless Community Leadership Collective, and their accounts were uniformly damning. Denton Hutchinson described a man lying dead in a recreation room for hours while security staff stationed feet away did nothing. Janet Jackson described being placed an hour and a half from her job in the Bronx after requesting something closer, returning alone at one or two in the morning to a desolate subway stop, and being ignored when she reported mice exacerbating her asthma. Francisco Ines, testifying through a translator, described being denied a room key in a unit designated for people with disabilities, having security open his door against his wishes, and being ignored when he filed a complaint. All three described a grievance system that was structurally broken: forms are obtained from the same staff against whom grievances are filed, complaints disappear, and residents fear retaliation for speaking up. One resident proposed a lockbox system for anonymous grievances. The Public Advocate confirmed there is no mechanism for making complaints that does not involve the very people complained about.

Commissioner Erin Dalton of the Department of Social Services led the administration's testimony, flanked by DHS First Deputy Commissioner Christine Fellini and Deputy Commissioner for Shelter Security Valvano Priester. Dalton opened by acknowledging that the procedures and protocols she was about to describe mean nothing without residents' trust, and that DHS does not currently measure resident perceptions of safety or dignity, but committed to building that capability. She presented data showing meaningful improvements across most safety metrics: serious violent incidents in single adult shelters fell 13% last year to 2.8 per thousand residents, down from 4.8 in fiscal year 2022; the shelter mortality rate fell by two-thirds in three years; and incident rates across all shelter types have roughly halved since fiscal year 2022. However, she flagged two areas of clear concern. Complaints about discrimination, assault and harassment rose 19% this year, and complaints about staff-client interactions rose 46%, which she interpreted as evidence that residents do not feel safe or respected, not merely as a data artifact. Flexible capacity shelters, mostly commercial hotels opened during the asylum-seeker surge, average approximately 42 open building violations per site compared to fewer than four in standard shelters, though officials noted some of this gap reflects applying residential code to buildings built under hotel code.

Council members pressed on several persistent structural problems. Chair Hudson focused heavily on conditions at the Tillery Street women's shelter in her district, where a provider change earlier in 2026 appears to have produced measurable improvements: priority one incidents down 45%, 911 calls down 13%, felony arrests down from 33 to 12. She raised a whistleblower complaint from a City employee alleging discrepancies between what was reported to DHS and what records showed, and that a security contractor retained against staff recommendations may warrant referral to the Department of Investigation. The administration confirmed DHS can terminate primary contracts and subcontracts, though the latter was initially stated incorrectly and corrected during the hearing. Council Member Morano pressed on community advisory boards being convened only after shelter siting decisions are made, and on the fact that safety transfers numbering 2,654 in the last fiscal year represent more than seven people moved per day for safety reasons, without any clear disciplinary consequence for repeat aggressors. Council Member Ung raised the ratio of housing specialists to residents, currently approximately 1 to 40, and the adequacy of staffing to move people out of the shelter system. Council Member Brewer pushed on school continuity, noting that only 62.9% of families are initially placed in the borough of their youngest school-aged child's school, and that absenteeism among shelter-dwelling children is likely being driven partly by transport failures. Council Member Avilés raised language access, confirming that DHS does not track the language capabilities of provider staff, relies on a language line rather than on-site bilingual staff, and cannot currently hold providers accountable for language competency. The administration acknowledged that only 82% of required security guards have completed mandatory 40-hour training, peace officer vacancies stand at 20%, and peace officer salaries are low enough relative to comparable City positions that retention is a chronic problem.

Both bills remain under consideration. The administration expressed general openness to working with sponsors on both, while flagging that shelter addresses must be protected, that zip code level placement data would require some rethinking of the existing MMR measure, and that domestic violence placements create legitimate reasons to place families away from their prior address that any reporting framework would need to accommodate.

▸ Numbers
  • 83,000 individuals slept in New York City shelters on the Sunday before the hearing, including 51,000 families with children.
  • DHS oversees 502 shelter sites, of which only 7 are directly operated by DHS.
  • 32 purpose-built shelters have been opened in the last four years, with an average capacity of approximately 160 residents.
  • Standard shelters average fewer than 4 open building violations each; flexible capacity shelters average approximately 42 open violations each.
  • Flexible capacity shelters number approximately 169 sites, mostly commercial hotels opened to meet asylum-seeker demand.
  • Serious violent incidents in single adult shelters fell 13% in fiscal year 2026 to 2.8 per thousand residents, down from 4.8 per thousand in fiscal year 2022.
  • The shelter system mortality rate fell by two-thirds in three years, from approximately 11 per thousand residents in fiscal year 2022 to 3.5 per thousand in fiscal year 2025; total deaths fell from approximately 500 to 305 while the shelter population nearly doubled.
  • Drug-related deaths account for 40% of all shelter deaths; 42 of 73 people who died in shelter in fiscal year 2025 died of an overdose. Drug-related deaths across the homeless population fell 28% last year.
  • Complaints about discrimination, assault and harassment rose 19% in fiscal year 2026; complaints about staff-client interactions rose 46%.
  • There were 2,654 safety transfers in fiscal year 2026, slightly up from 2,625 in fiscal year 2025.
  • Serious incident rates per thousand residents across shelter types: single adults fell from 54.7 in fiscal year 2022 to 28.3 in fiscal year 2026; adult families fell from 21.8 in fiscal year 2023 to 9.0 in fiscal year 2026; families with children fell from 16.0 in fiscal year 2022 to 8.0 in fiscal year 2026.
  • 62.9% of families are initially placed in the borough of their youngest school-aged child's school; over 80% are in that borough over the duration of their shelter stay.
  • 31,620 complaints were entered into the IQ complaint system in fiscal year 2026; average resolution time is 5 days.
  • 268 new capital needs are pending across 95 privately operated shelters in various stages of review.
  • DHS has 55 City- and State-owned shelter buildings; the fiscal year 2027 capital budget includes $106 million for 102 projects across 38 shelters, with 8 complete and 31 in active construction.
  • DHS has 352 peace officers deployed across 18 shelter sites, in staffing ranges of 10 to 26 per site; the current peace officer vacancy rate is 20%, down from 25%.
  • 3,542 total security guard positions exist across the shelter system; 82% (2,916) have completed the required 40-hour training; 542 have completed the required annual 8-hour refresher.
  • The ratio of housing specialists to shelter residents is approximately 1 to 40.
  • 113 of 143 targeted family shelter sites (79%) have at least one mental health professional on site as required by Local Law 35 of 2023.
  • The DHS ombudsman's office has 12 filled positions with a 13th onboarding, and contacts approximately 90% of complainants upon receipt of their complaint.
  • At Tillery Street women's shelter, following a provider change in 2026: priority one incidents down 45%, 911 calls down 13% to 1,248 (lowest since 2022), felony arrests down from 33 to 12.
  • DHS security budget for fiscal year 2027 includes approximately $18 million for peace officers plus contracted security costs; the full breakdown was not available at the hearing.
  • Model budget staffing for a 200-bed shelter: 43 staff for single adults, 46 for single adults with behavioral health services, 51 at assessment sites, 60 for adult families, 37 for families with children.
▸ Action Points
  • DHS to provide the Council with the legal analysis underlying the City's decision to decline the VA grant for homeless veterans, with a written timeline for delivery.
  • DHS to provide demographic trend data on older adults and individuals with substance use disorder in the shelter system over time, specifically how those shares have changed.
  • DHS to provide clarification on the apparent inconsistency between mortality data showing both a growing share of children and a growing share of older adults in the shelter system.
  • DHS to build and implement a direct, technology-enabled channel for regular resident feedback on safety, dignity and conditions in shelters, as committed to by Commissioner Dalton.
  • DHS to investigate the whistleblower complaint described by Chair Hudson regarding discrepancies between reported data and observed records at a shelter, and the retention of a security subcontractor against staff recommendations, including potential referral to the Department of Investigation.
  • DHS to provide the Council with a full breakdown of the security budget for fiscal year 2026 and fiscal year 2027, including expenditure against the $313 million figure cited by the committee.
  • DHS to provide the Council with data on the number of housing specialists citywide and performance metrics for housing specialist roles.
  • DHS to follow up with the Council on the number of school-focused case managers in the shelter system and current data-sharing arrangements with New York City Public Schools on transportation and attendance.
  • DHS to provide data on telehealth utilization by shelter residents under Local Law 35 of 2023.
  • DHS to investigate whether shelter budgets have in fact been adjusted to cover mental health professional requirements under Local Law 35, given provider complaints that no budget increase accompanied the mandate.
  • DHS to provide the Council with whistleblower policy documentation, including how it is communicated to provider staff and subcontractors, and data on the number of whistleblower complaints received.
  • DHS to provide the Council with data on how retaliation protections are communicated to shelter residents, and to examine whether those protections are adequate.
  • DHS to track and report subcontractor-specific complaints separately in its data systems, and to clarify in writing the full extent of DHS authority to cancel subcontracts.
  • DHS to provide the Council with a standardised information packet covering resident rights, the grievance process, good neighbor policies and whistleblower protections, suitable for distribution through Council offices.
  • DHS to provide the Council with data on the annual 8-hour refresher training completion rate for security guards once enhanced tracking is in place.
  • DHS to report on compliance with sanctuary city training requirements, including frequency of refresher training and provider compliance rates.
  • DHS to explore with relevant City and State partners the reclassification and pay equity of DHS peace officer positions relative to comparable roles at other City agencies.
  • DHS to report on the feasibility of reporting family shelter placements at zip code level, and to engage with Council Member Brewer's office and the Public Advocate's office on the specific measures and geographies in both Int 0099-2026 and Int 0387-2026.
  • DHS to provide the Council with formal planning documentation on coordination between DHS and MOCJ regarding housing and shelter access for individuals exiting Rikers Island, including data on how many people exiting custody enter DHS shelters.
  • Chair Hudson to invite Commissioner Dalton and relevant DHS officials to visit Tillery Street women's shelter.
  • Chair Hudson to raise DHS peace officer pay equity with the Mayor and the Governor.
