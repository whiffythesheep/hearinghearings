An Overview of the Parks Department's Events Permitting Process

Committee on Parks and Recreation

Chair: Ty Hankerson
September 30, 2026 · 1hr 44m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Parks and Recreation held an oversight hearing on the Parks Department's special events permitting process, alongside consideration of Int 0927-2026, a street renaming bill co-sponsored by Council Members Brooks-Powers and Schulman that would restore the original name Edgemere Avenue to a stretch of Shorefront Parkway in Queens, resolving longstanding mail delivery confusion. The renaming bill generated no controversy and was handled briefly. The bulk of the hearing focused on how Parks manages the roughly 24,000 to 25,000 special event permits it processes annually, what the costs and benefits are to parkland and communities, and whether the process is sufficiently navigable for ordinary New Yorkers and community-based organizations.

Deputy Commissioner David Sarah and his colleagues testified that the permitting system is broadly functional: outright denials run below 1% of applications in every recent year, 75% of events are for under 100 attendees, and 95% are for under 500. Parks described a tiered system in which small events require only a $25 non-refundable fee, while larger events trigger additional requirements including insurance, security bonds, site fees, and ancillary permits from agencies such as NYPD, FDNY, DOB and DOHMH. For very large events, inter-agency coordination involves the Mayor's Office and can encompass multiple planning meetings covering security, transportation, promotion and performer vetting. The department also noted recent process improvements including expanded early application windows, a redesigned website, a bilingual informational pamphlet, and centralization of the citywide special events policy office.

Members pressed on several substantive gaps. Chair Hankerson raised the problem of late-stage permit decisions leaving small community organizations in limbo, questioned why permit offices have uneven staffing across boroughs (notably Brooklyn's reduced headcount following retirements), and asked why applicants for back-to-school giveaways are asked about the monetary value of donated backpacks, a question the department struggled to justify convincingly. He also identified a significant structural inefficiency: applicants needing permits for food, amplified sound, merchandise sales and a Parks permit must submit separate applications to multiple agencies with no centralized portal, and asked whether Parks had explored working with the city's technology office to consolidate this. The department acknowledged it had not yet initiated that conversation. Council Member Morano raised a pointed and largely unanswered question about who in the permitting process represents displaced ordinary park users — the person who shows up to find their usual field or path blocked — rather than the applicant seeking the permit, and asked whether Parks tracks the total park-days lost to events including setup and breakdown time. The department acknowledged it does not systematically track displacement or total access loss, though it noted that permitted events often occupy only part of a park.

Council Member Ung surfaced the newly introduced $721 fee for use of a mobile stage (winger wagon), which the department explained was driven by the operational cost of pulling specialized staff for full days of transport duty and the consequent need for overtime. The threshold triggering additional permitting requirements for bouncy houses was confirmed to have been recently lowered from 500 to 200 attendees, following Council feedback about safety incidents. Council Member Sanchez, drawing on her pre-Council experience applying for parks permits, pushed for clearer status communication during the review process, more structured office-hours-style sessions where organizations can sit with Parks staff to learn the process directly, and better outreach for the partner input forums Parks has been running. She also raised the issue of organizations that comply with permit conditions finding unpermitted events with prohibited equipment happening simultaneously in the same park, with no clear complaints mechanism beyond 311. The department conceded this is a gap and committed to examining it. On enforcement against bad actors, the department described a system of violation letters, photo documentation and permit suspensions of one to two years for egregious cases, but acknowledged there is no formal rule-based framework for tracking problematic event promoters across years.

  • Parks processed 23,948 special event permits in 2022, of which 47 were denied.
  • Parks processed 23,844 special event permits in 2023, of which 18 were denied.
  • Parks processed 23,507 special event permits in 2024, of which 37 were denied.
  • Parks processed 24,241 special event permits in 2025, of which 23 were denied.
  • Parks is tracking slightly above 25,000 permit applications for 2026.
  • Outright denials represent less than 1% of all permits processed in each recent year.
  • Any event with more than 20 attendees requires a special event permit.
  • The non-refundable permit application processing fee is $25.
  • Events expected to exceed 500 attendees are handled by the Citywide Special Events team rather than borough offices.
  • 75% of permitted events are for 100 attendees or fewer.
  • 95% of permitted events are for 500 attendees or fewer.
  • Permit applications for typical events must be submitted at least 21 days in advance.
  • Parks now allows permit applications to be submitted starting the second Tuesday after Labor Day each year, enabling applications up to 14 months in advance for future-year events.
  • Approximately 20% of annual permits are processed in roughly 8% of the calendar year during peak periods.
  • Borough permit office staffing: Staten Island 3 coordinators, Queens 6, Manhattan 8, Brooklyn 5, Bronx 8.
  • The Citywide Special Events team numbers 8 staff.
  • Nine partner input forums were held across all five boroughs in early 2025, with over 140 distinct partner groups participating.
  • The fee for use of a Parks winger wagon (mobile stage) is $721, introduced in the prior fiscal year.
  • The attendee threshold triggering additional permitting requirements for bouncy houses was recently reduced from 500 to 200.
  • Large events with egregious violations can result in permit suspensions of up to two years; standard violations typically result in a one-year suspension.
  • Parks requires large-scale events to have a private meteorologist on site; there is reportedly one former New York-based meteorologist who regularly performs this function.

The occurrence of cultural and recreational programming such as art festivals, films and concerts have always been a core feature of the City park system. Most of these events are free and sometimes run by Parks itself or through public-private partners. However, some of these events are also pay-to-enter events that include thousands of participants and are often run by event promoters that have a permit with Parks. There is a multifaceted process for approving these various events. For example, any event with more than 20 participants at minimum requires a special event permit for a nonrefundable $25 fee. As the event grows more complex, so does the process. In the case of large events that involve more than 500 participants or may be commercial in nature, Parks imposes additional requirements that must be fulfilled before a permit is issued. These can include fees based on the concession fee schedule and additional insurance or bonding obligations. Furthermore, depending on the scope, location and size of the event, additional permits from agencies like the Department of Buildings or the Police Department for potential security concerns may be necessary.

Aside from the substance of the event, other requirements can factor in. If food and merchandise is expected to be sold, a simple use authorization permit is required. A fee for this permit is negotiated with Parks based on several factors, including the number of vendors at the event, estimated attendance and the prices of the items being sold. Once all the requirements have been met by the applicant, Parks will issue a special event permit at their discretion. The complexity of this process can sometimes be problematic from both the side of the event applicant and the Parks Department, as it must walk a fine line towards maintaining a vibrant parks environment that also prioritizes equitable access to all New Yorkers.

Event applicants, especially smaller community-based ones, often cite issues with not being able to fully understand what is required to secure a permit and not understanding the reasons as to why they have been denied. Historically, there have been concerns raised about the amount of parkland used for large ticketed events, the amount of revenue being generated for the event and the condition of parkland left after the event concludes. Damage resulting from large crowds, vehicles, vendors and bad weather can render parkland unusable for extended periods of time. This has left many community members concerned that some large and commercial events can deprive New Yorkers of quality access to green space.

I hope this hearing results in having a better understanding of how Parks goes about approving special events, what the overall costs and benefits are to the City's open space and whether there are ways we can improve the process to ensure that events are accessible to all and are always respectful to the parks where they occur and to the communities they occur in.

Our permitting staff works directly with event organizers to make their events a reality while ensuring that proposed events are appropriate and avoid negative impacts on our beloved open spaces. Any park event expected to have more than 20 attendees is required to have a special event permit, though permits are also available for groups of any size hoping to reserve a specific area in a park. Anyone can apply for a special event permit from the agency, from organizations planning a large event for the public to families looking to host smaller political parties, weddings, unions or barbecues. It is this latter category of smaller events that makes up the vast majority of permit requests submitted to the agency.

Permit applications are typically submitted through a dedicated page on our agency website. There is a $25 processing fee for permit applications, which allows the City to recoup some of the costs of processing applications and helps discourage attempts to monopolize permits citywide. For the majority of permit requests, this nominal fee is the only cost for an applicant to be able to hold their event. Most special event permit requests are processed by staff in our permit offices in each of the five boroughs. To ensure there is ample time to properly review and discuss proposed event details with all necessary stakeholders, which might include other City agencies or construction contractors working on site, we require permit requests for typical permit events to be submitted at least 21 days in advance. The duration of the request review for each permit, from submission to approval, can vary depending on the scope and complexity of the event being submitted and the volume of permit applications being processed by the permit offices. Similarly, it is not uncommon for an event proposal to evolve and iterate as event organizers provide more information or as the review requires additional dialogue with other entities, such as the use of amplified sound for an event, which requires NYPD approval.

As such, we ask that people apply with as much lead time as possible to ensure they can secure their desired dates, and keep in mind that the final permit approvals and issuances are typically not issued until closer to the event date. However, the agency does everything in its power to expedite the review and approval of permit requests, and even though applicants are asked to submit requests at least 21 days in advance, this does not mean that a given request will take that long to approve.

We are pleased to report that outright denials of special event permits are exceedingly rare, making up less than 1% of the permits processed by the agency. In the event that a proposed event location is already permitted for another event, we are nearly always able to work with the applicant to identify alternative locations or support dates for their event to carry forward.

Though the majority of our thousands of special event permits each year are issued for small picnics, barbecues and birthday parties, our parks also serve as the location for larger events with a more expansive reach, such as concerts and set-up festivals. Though these larger events present additional considerations for the agency's review, we feel that activating our parks with a wide range of recreational and cultural activity helps make them more robust and dynamic public spaces for everyone to enjoy.

To ensure that larger event permit requests can be handled consistently and fairly, any event expected to have over 500 attendees is permitted through our Citywide Special Events team. Given that these events are considerably more complex than a typical small event, potentially disrupting access to a park or even resulting in damage to the park, permit applicants for large events need to provide additional information to the agency, including a preliminary plan, preliminary site map and production schedule, proof of commercial general liability insurance, submission of a security bond to be held against damages incurred and payment of a site fee on the concession fee schedule established by the rules. Additionally, depending on the proposed components of the event, applicants need to secure ancillary permits from other City agencies, such as the Fire Department for use of a generator, the Department of Buildings for installation of a large structure, the Police Department for safety or the Department of Health and Mental Hygiene for food distribution.

We collaborate often with our sister agencies and are in constant communication with them when it comes to moving permit applications forward. We always try to work with applicants toward an outcome that works for everyone. As the partner agency ultimately responsible for the care and protection of our City parkland and all New Yorkers, we are not able to accommodate every aspect of each request for each park. If the requested location is inappropriate for the proposed event activity because of the park size, configuration or natural features, or if the proposed activity is likely to harm the park or significantly interfere with the enjoyment of the park by other users, then the agency has grounds to deny the permit application, but this is relatively rare.
