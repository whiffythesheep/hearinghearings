What's Next for CreateNYC?

Committee on Cultural Affairs, Libraries and International Relations

Chair: Nantasha M. Williams
October 19, 2026 · 3hrs 31m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Cultural Affairs, Libraries and International Relations held an oversight hearing on October 19, 2026 titled "What's Next for CreateNYC," examining whether the city's 2017 cultural plan has delivered on its promises nearly a decade after adoption. The committee also heard four resolutions: Proposed Res. 163-A designating the fourth week of November as Shangri-Las Week; Res. 164-2026 calling on the state legislature to designate Kwanzaa as a public holiday; Res. 186-2026 designating November 28 as Albanian Independence Day; and Proposed Res. 625-A declaring October as Reggaeton Heritage Month, the last of which drew passionate testimony from Panamanian community advocates about the genre's Afro-Caribbean roots and New York City's role in its development.

The substantive core of the hearing was a rigorous if sometimes frustrating accounting of CreateNYC's performance. University of Pennsylvania researchers Mark Stern and Susan Seifert from the Social Impact of the Arts Project presented findings from a decade-long study showing that while cultural assets have shifted geographically from Manhattan toward Brooklyn and Queens, overall inequality in their distribution has actually widened since 2016. Their key finding was that lower-income neighborhoods with dense cultural assets show measurably better health outcomes, school performance, and social connection than comparable neighborhoods without them, making cultural investment a public health and equity issue, not merely an amenity. They introduced the concept of "civic clusters," lower-income neighborhoods that punch above their weight culturally, and argued the city should build outward from these strengths rather than continue a top-down funding model. The Independent Budget Office's Arjun Armbrewster presented a new report on the Cultural Development Fund finding that 60 percent of grantees are based in Manhattan and receive 63.5 percent of funding, that half of all grantees have budgets under half a million dollars with two or fewer full-time employees, and that two-thirds serve historically underserved communities. He flagged that Manhattan-based organizations do not plan programs outside the borough at higher rates than outer-borough grantees, suggesting the geographic equity problem is structural.

Department of Cultural Affairs Commissioner EJ Diaw gave a candid if sometimes evasive accounting of the plan's record. Genuine achievements include the near-doubling of the Cultural Development Fund from roughly 40 million dollars to 74.3 million dollars and the expansion of grantees from 950 to 1,707 organizations, the launch of the Disability Forward Fund and Language Access Fund, the Culture Pass program generating over 568,000 reservations, the CUNY Cultural Core internship program placing over 1,100 students, and the Create in Place initiative supporting over 1,000 organizations navigate real estate challenges. Admitted shortcomings include a living wage strategy that produced almost no measurable impact, a charter-mandated interagency coordination plan that appears not to have been produced in any meaningful form, key performance indicators whose public data stopped at 2022 until the department quietly uploaded updates the weekend before the hearing, mandatory biennial reports that were apparently never submitted to the Mayor or the Speaker as the City Charter requires, and a Cultural Affairs Advisory Commission whose website still lists members who may no longer serve. Chair Williams pressed the commissioner repeatedly on accountability gaps, particularly around the CUNY Cultural Core pipeline where nine years of data on outcomes for 1,100 interns have not been systematically tracked, and around the absence of a granular assessment of which specific communities remain underserved.

Public testimony was extensive and consistent in its themes. Organizations including the Bronx Arts Ensemble, the Asian American Arts Alliance, Lincoln Center, the Public Theater, the Entertainment Community Fund, ArtBuilt, and India's Space all emphasized the accelerating affordability crisis for artists and cultural workers, the lack of portable health insurance for freelance teaching artists, the absence of permanent affordable cultural workspace, and the need to embed cultural infrastructure requirements into rezoning and development processes from the outset rather than as afterthoughts. Disability access advocates, including representatives of the Cultural Accessibility in New York City research project, described the Disability Forward Fund as opaque and underanalyzed, and called for disabled artists and activists to be brought back into decision-making. National Queer Theater testified about the direct impact of federal funding revocations on queer arts organizations and called for a dedicated five million dollar baseline fund for queer arts. The Culture and Arts Policy Institute proposed a structural reform: separating a capacity-building plan for the Department of Cultural Affairs from a broader sector-wide shared agenda convened jointly by the department and the Council, arguing CreateNYC failed in part because it was asked to be both simultaneously. A citizen-researcher testified about filing an Article 78 after years of unanswered FOIL requests regarding the She Built NYC statue program, only to be told by Cultural Affairs that nothing had happened, which the chair directly contradicted from her own experience with the Billie Holiday statue in her district.

▸ Numbers
  • The Cultural Development Fund distributed 74.3 million dollars to 1,707 cultural groups in fiscal year 2026, up from just over 40 million dollars to 950 groups when CreateNYC was released in 2017.
  • Since 2018, more than 21.7 million dollars of CDF funding has been allocated to groups in underserved neighborhoods.
  • A 2019 arrangement with the Met Museum provided an additional 2.8 million dollars distributed to 275 groups across the Bronx and Queens.
  • 602 organizations are currently in a multi-year funding cycle, more than half of the total grantee pool.
  • The Disability Forward Fund and Language Access Fund together have provided more than 700 grants totaling over 7 million dollars; a separate figure of 600 grants totaling over 6 million dollars was also cited for these funds.
  • The Culture Pass program has generated over 568,000 reservations to cultural institutions for New York City residents.
  • A related program has provided 806,000 free cultural memberships; approximately 5,000 of those were one-year memberships at at least one cultural organization.
  • The CUNY Cultural Core program has placed over 1,100 CUNY students in paid internships at cultural institutions since its launch, with no comprehensive outcome tracking conducted by the Department of Cultural Affairs.
  • The city's record-setting cultural budget allocation for fiscal year 2027 is 323 million dollars, including a 10 million dollar cultural stability fund.
  • Individual artist grant awards were doubled from 5,000 dollars to 10,000 dollars, the first increase in nearly 20 years.
  • The affordable real estate for artists program created 165 units of affordable workspace using 10 million dollars in capital funds before being sunsetted.
  • 5,000 square feet was secured at the Brooklyn Army Terminal, giving more than 100 artists access to long-term affordable workspace.
  • Arts education funding in New York City public schools increased from 516 million dollars in fiscal year 2024 to approximately 563 million dollars in fiscal year 2025.
  • The City Council provided nearly 4 million dollars in grants to 223 schools across all council districts for arts education in the most recent school year; 369 organizations serving K-12 students received council grants in 2024.
  • 300 free field trips to Title I schools are offered annually through the Department of Cultural Affairs.
  • Create in Place has supported more than 1,000 creative small businesses and nonprofit arts organizations and reached more than 800 people since its 2024 launch.
  • The Office of Nightlife has resolved hundreds of quality-of-life and venue safety cases since 2018.
  • 60 percent of CDF grantees are based in Manhattan, and 63.5 percent of CDF funding goes to those grantees.
  • Half of CDF grantees have budgets under 500,000 dollars with two or fewer full-time employees; the smallest grantees receive approximately 50 percent of their revenue from government sources, compared to around 10 percent for the largest.
  • Two-thirds of CDF grantees serve historically underserved communities as defined by CreateNYC.
  • 43 percent of Manhattan-based CDF grantees planned programs outside of Manhattan; 74 percent of all grantees planned programs in Manhattan.
  • 70 percent of Bronx-based CDF grantees planned programs only within the Bronx.
  • Asian American and Pacific Islander-led and serving organizations represent 18 percent of New York City's population but received only 5 percent of CDF grants, with average grant amounts 28 percent below the citywide average.
  • Cultural workers in New York City earn 23 percent less than the national average when adjusted for cost of living.
  • Arts and culture generate over 10 billion dollars annually and employ over 325,000 New Yorkers; tourism related to the sector generates an additional 74 billion dollars in economic impact.
  • TDF's Tickets for Teens program gave away 20,000 theater tickets to high schoolers this year; over 23,000 students signed up in five days and unlocked or purchased over 5,000 admissions.
  • In fiscal year 2022, city agencies and public libraries spent at least 89 million dollars on cultural programming, more than double the approximately 39 million dollars in competitive CDF grants that year, with no coordinated tracking of that spending.
  • 69 percent of eighth-grade students are failing to meet state arts education requirements, largely due to a shortage of certified arts teachers.
  • Over 900 cultural organizations partnered with New York City public schools last school year, serving 84 percent of schools.
  • Create in Place secured loans for 75 artists and small businesses to recover following the Red Hook fire.
  • 39 cultural organizations are currently members of the Cultural Institutions Group, the largest expansion since the 1970s having added five members, one per borough.
  • The number of concentrated disadvantaged neighborhoods in New York City fell from 65 in 2000 to 52 in 2025, with over half a million fewer New Yorkers living in such neighborhoods.
▸ Action Points
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to submit the 2023 and 2025 biennial CreateNYC progress reports to the Mayor and the Speaker of the City Council as required by the City Charter.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to publish updated CreateNYC key performance indicator data for years beyond 2022 on the agency's website and keep it current on a fiscal-year basis.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to publish a full written accounting of which CreateNYC commitments were completed, modified, abandoned, or never attempted, in connection with the potential plan update.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to establish a baseline granular assessment of which specific communities and neighborhoods are least served by city cultural funding, with an end-of-2027 target.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to conduct a comprehensive outcome study of the CUNY Cultural Core program to track the career trajectories of the 1,100-plus interns who have participated since 2017.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to publish the findings from its ongoing commissioner listening tour across all five boroughs, with the commissioner committing to synthesize transcripts and notes and share publicly.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to appoint new members to the Cultural Affairs Advisory Commission and restore accurate membership information to its public website within approximately one month.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to publish an enhanced open data set for the Cultural Development Fund including funding amounts and geographic information.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to publish a formal review of all application and reporting requirements to simplify processes for small grantees, as committed in the racial equity plan.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to follow up directly with CM Marte's office regarding the relocation of the Elizabeth Jennings Graham monument to lower Manhattan, and to explore integration with the ongoing DOT redesign of Chatham Square.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs commissioner to meet with the Cultural Accessibility in New York City project steering committee and to connect them with the Speaker's Disability Committee.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to brief the chair's office on the interagency coordination plan required under the City Charter, which the commissioner appeared to concede has not been produced in full.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to follow up with the author of a multi-year FOIL request regarding the She Built NYC women's statue program to provide a substantive update on program status.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to develop a plan for mid-career cultural workers to advance into senior leadership and board governance roles, building on and scaling CUNY Cultural Core models.
  • Department of Cultural Affairs to deepen collaboration with the Department of Education arts office, including regular structured meetings, to advance the CreateNYC objective of high-quality arts education for all public school students.
  • Chair Williams to work with the Cultural Accessibility in New York City project to convene a roundtable including the Department of Cultural Affairs and other relevant agencies on disability access in the cultural sector.
  • Chair Williams to continue internal work on a package of cultural legislation including artist preference housing, portable health benefits for teaching artists, and cultural infrastructure requirements in rezoning processes.
  • Chair Williams to follow up with the Commissioner regarding the status of the She Built NYC statue for Billie Holiday in her district and the broader program timeline.
  • City Council committee staff to coordinate with the Independent Budget Office for a detailed walkthrough of its new CDF grantee analysis report series.
  • Independent Budget Office to publish the remaining reports in its CDF grantee analysis series and to host a webinar on cultural policy and public administration.
