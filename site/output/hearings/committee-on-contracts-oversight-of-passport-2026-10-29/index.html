Oversight of PASSPort

Committee on Contracts

Chair: Lincoln Restler
October 29, 2026 · 2hrs 6m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Contracts Committee held a wide-ranging oversight hearing on PASSPort, the City's procurement and contracting platform, alongside consideration of four pieces of legislation and a resolution aimed at reforming small capital project delivery. The hearing featured testimony from city officials including the MOCS director, a DDC commissioner, and public witnesses from the procurement reform space and the Human Services Council. The committee, led by Chair Restler, probed PASSPort's costs, staffing, system performance, user experience, and future direction with more rigor than is typical of oversight hearings, while also pushing on capital project inefficiencies through the legislative agenda.

On PASSPort, the picture that emerged was of a system that processes an enormous volume of city contracting activity, roughly $40-45 billion annually, at a cost of around $10 million per year in vendor contracts, but which remains deeply frustrating for end users, particularly nonprofits. The MOCS director was candid about gaps and turbulence caused by rapid system changes, and acknowledged a very long internal wish list of improvements that the agency cannot fully fund or implement. The committee focused heavily on the experience of nonprofit providers, with Chair Restler describing firsthand a visit to a local nonprofit where a single invoice submission took a staff member half a day, requiring manual transcription of dozens of fields from spreadsheets into PASSPort with no bulk upload functionality, no recognition of prior data, and inconsistent field layouts across agencies. Council Member Brewer reinforced this, noting that some providers are so frustrated they simply choose not to work with the city, potentially excluding high-quality organizations. Release Seven of PASSPort, due in early 2027 at a cost of $6.5 million, was highlighted as a critical near-term fix, particularly for the longstanding bug that blocks invoice submission while a budget modification is pending. The Human Services Council testified that this particular flaw is not a policy decision but a system quirk that has trapped nonprofits in impossible cash-flow situations for years, with some owed retroactive COLAs and indirect cost rate adjustments. The MOCS director confirmed the scope of the problem, noting that 10,000 budget modifications occurred in the last fiscal year, each blocking invoicing for an average of five days.

Structural questions about PASSPort's future were among the more substantive moments of the hearing. The committee raised the issue of capital eligibility: because the city licenses rather than owns PASSPort, improvements cannot be capitalized, making sustained investment harder to justify in budget terms. The MOCS director floated two conceptual options: purchasing the intellectual property outright from software vendor InValue, which has built city-specific customizations on top of its platform, or eventually commissioning a city-owned system built by city technologists. Both were acknowledged as very early-stage ideas. The committee also questioned why OTI, the city's central technology office, is not more directly involved in day-to-day PASSPort management, given that the system processes tens of billions of dollars and has over 123,000 agency users. The MOCS director said OTI's involvement has recently increased at a strategic level and that deeper involvement would be welcomed. On the question of DOE procuring its own separate system rather than adopting PASSPort, the MOCS director said she was unaware of those conversations having included MOCS, which the committee found unsatisfying.

The legislative agenda covered four bills and a resolution on small capital projects. On Intro 1064, which would impose timeframes on agency approvals and change orders, a DDC official acknowledged that the main delay is not paperwork but the back-and-forth with contractors over whether something constitutes a change order at all, which can take four to five months before any formal clock starts. He expressed willingness to set timelines once agreement is reached, noting the formal process takes roughly two months. On Intro 1065, which would require cost benchmarks for small recurring capital projects, the official expressed comfort with publishing historical contract data but concern about forward-looking cost benchmarks given construction cost escalation running at around 8% annually and market volatility driven by factors like data center construction absorbing raw materials. On Intro 0991, which would require a catalog of standard designs, he drew a distinction between standard designs, which he opposed as too inflexible given site-specific infrastructure requirements, and design guidelines, which he strongly endorsed, citing five Parks Department bathrooms being delivered in 18 months at $3.6 million each using strict guidelines. A resolution calling on the state to lower the threshold for design-build was also on the agenda, with design-build flagged as a best-value procurement method that avoids lowest-bidder problems. Public witness Charles Diamond argued for more investment in procurement infrastructure overall and urged a yes vote on Ballot Question Two, which would allow the Mayor to delegate approval of non-competitively bid contracts over $5 million to agency chief contracting officers and make permanent the public comment period in place of a public hearing. The MOCS director supported both elements of the ballot question.

▸ Numbers
  • PASSPort processes approximately $40-45 billion in city contracting transactions annually.
  • The city has spent at least $120 million on PASSPort since its inception in 2016, including $9.7 million with software vendor InValue and over $20 million with Accenture.
  • The current steady-state annual cost of PASSPort is approximately $10 million: $2.5 million per year to InValue for software licenses and $7.5 million per year to Accenture as systems integrator.
  • Release Seven of PASSPort, due in early 2027, is budgeted at $6.5 million.
  • Both the InValue and Accenture contracts are set to expire in April 2027 with no renewal option, requiring a new procurement or negotiated acquisition.
  • Approximately 32 to 40 MOCS technology staff support PASSPort, representing roughly 20-30% or more of MOCS total operational headcount.
  • MOCS headcount is approximately 170 positions below its authorized level.
  • PASSPort currently has 34,000 registered vendors and 123,000 active agency users; approximately 87,000 used the system in the last fiscal year.
  • To date, PASSPort has processed 35,330 registered contracts and 22,605 amendments with a combined value of $187 billion; 472,000 invoices have been dispersed.
  • In fiscal year 2026, 135,000 invoices were created in PASSPort, a 12% increase over the prior year.
  • PASSPort system uptime was approximately 99.1% or higher in the review period.
  • There are approximately $1 billion in unpaid invoices currently visible in the PASSPort queue, though not all are late.
  • Average invoice payment time has fallen from 41 days to approximately 22 days, below the 30-day Procurement Policy Board benchmark.
  • In fiscal year 2026, 69% of contracts were registered on time; in FY25 approximately 28% were registered after their start date (72% late by one reading of the data, though the committee sought written clarification).
  • The median cycle time for competitive sealed proposals rose from 386 days in FY25 to 527 days in FY26; the median for competitive sealed bids declined year over year.
  • There were approximately 10,000 budget modifications in the last fiscal year, each blocking invoice submission in PASSPort for an average of five days.
  • Five Parks Department bathrooms built under design guidelines cost $3.6 million each and are being delivered in approximately 18 months, roughly twice as fast as traditional design timelines.
  • Construction cost escalation is running at approximately 8% per year, driven in part by data center construction absorbing steel and concrete supply.
  • A new bathroom at Kissena Park in Flushing requires approximately half a mile of deep sewer and electrical infrastructure to connect to the street, a major driver of costs.
  • The Ballot Question Two reform package was estimated by the Charter Revision Commission to save one to two weeks on approvals for non-competitive contracts over $5 million, and up to 30 days overall on a procurement.
  • Public hearings on contracts, which the ballot question would replace with public comment periods, are held less than once a month, primarily for shelter contracts.
  • MOCS annual budget allocation for PASSPort maintenance is approximately $14 million this year, down from $22 million the prior year, representing 0.025% of annual contract spend.
▸ Action Points
  • MOCS to provide the committee in writing with a precise count of full-time employee equivalents dedicated to PASSPort support.
  • MOCS to rerun and share updated data on unregistered contracts and unpaid invoice backlog, as the Comptroller's April 2025 report is now over a year old.
  • MOCS to provide written FY26 indicators on contract registration timeliness and invoice payment cycle times for comparison with prior years.
  • MOCS to follow up with the committee on the number of non-competitively bid contracts over $5 million to give a more precise estimate of the scope of Ballot Question Two's delegation provision.
  • MOCS to estimate the cost of adding simultaneous OMB and Law Department review functionality to PASSPort and report back to the committee.
  • MOCS to take back the question of whether PASSPort and PIP currently communicate effectively and report to the committee.
  • MOCS to engage OTI more directly in PASSPort management, with Chair Restler indicating he would raise greater OTI involvement with the relevant city technology leadership.
  • MOCS to share its internal post-Release Seven roadmap options, including deprecation of functionality and potential acquisition of intellectual property, with the committee once Release Seven is delivered.
  • MOCS to engage with the Comptroller's office and the Law Department on capital eligibility for PASSPort improvements, with Chair Restler offering to help facilitate those conversations.
  • MOCS to work with Council Finance on contingency protections for discretionary grant pilot participants at risk of losing funding due to paperwork failures rather than non-performance.
  • MOCS to investigate whether contractor-controlled or wrap-up insurance programs used in construction contexts could be adapted to reduce insurance burdens on nonprofit human services providers.
  • DDC to share information from the restarted Capital Reform Task Force with the committee once finalized, including any internal deadlines agencies have set for change order processing.
  • DDC to develop more proactive capital planning communication with elected officials so that budget gaps between initial estimates and scoped project costs are surfaced earlier and not attributed to the agency or elected official after funding has been committed.
  • MOCS and the committee to convene a joint session with nonprofit and vendor stakeholders post-Release Seven to identify priority reforms to PASSPort from the user perspective.
  • MOCS to confirm in writing whether any mayoral agencies continue to operate legacy procurement systems in parallel with PASSPort.
  • Human Services Council to continue engagement with the committee on Release Seven rollout and its effect on the budget-mod invoice-blocking issue.
