Local Law to Amend the Administrative Code of the City of New York, in Relation to Pedicabs

Committee on Consumer and Worker Protection

Chair: Harvey D. Epstein
October 7, 2026 · 2hrs 28m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Consumer and Worker Protection, chaired by Harvey Epstein and effectively co-led by veteran pedicab reformer CM Gale Brewer, held a hearing on seven bills aimed at overhauling New York City's thoroughly dysfunctional pedicab industry. The hearing grew directly out of a 2025-2026 reform process convened by the Midtown Community Justice Center, whose director Daniel Modest testified alongside industry representatives, BID leaders and the Central Park Conservancy. The basic facts of the situation are not in dispute by anyone: the City has an 840-vehicle cap on registered pedicabs, only about 330 are actually registered, and an estimated 1,200 to 1,500 are operating illegally. Three quarters of the industry is unlicensed, enforcement is nearly completely ineffective, and tourists are routinely charged hundreds or even over a thousand dollars for twenty-minute rides with no meaningful recourse.

The legislative package addresses this comprehensively. Int 0993-2026 would transfer regulatory authority from the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection to the Taxi and Limousine Commission. Int 0992-2026 would require meters, scannable registration plates and driver safety training, and would remove the possibility of criminal imprisonment for violations. Int 0673-2026 would set fixed per-minute fares indexed to inflation. Int 0077-2026 would require the ride timer to be visible to passengers. Int 1063-2026 would legalize electric pedal assist up to 750 watts with a 15mph cap. Int 1026-2026 would create designated curb spaces for pedicabs. Int 0064-2026 would restrict pedicab operations within 50 feet of theater entrances in the Theater District. The Broadway Association, the Central Park Conservancy, the Times Square Alliance, the Midtown Community Justice Center and the New York Pedicab Alliance all broadly supported the package, which is a notably rare alignment of neighborhood interests and industry advocacy. The industry representatives were especially emphatic about legalizing electric assist, with multiple drivers testifying about physical injuries and the practical reality that virtually all pedicabs already operate with motors regardless of the current ban.

The most contested bill is Int 0993-2026, the TLC transfer. DCWP was diplomatically equivocal, acknowledging the system is broken while flagging implementation concerns. The TLC was more pointed in its opposition, noting it has no expertise in the pedicab industry, no existing regulatory framework that maps onto it, significant resource constraints following recent budget cuts, and a large existing caseload of 180,000 licensed for-hire drivers. TLC Commissioner Paul Bader testified that 64 enforcement officers had been lost to budget cuts in the last fiscal year, and noted that NYPD officers working Midtown have told him they would prefer not to handle pedicab regulation. Despite the TLC's reluctance, the non-governmental witnesses and both sponsoring Council Members were clear that the status quo of divided, unconnected licensing and enforcement authority is the root cause of the compliance failure, and that TLC-style integrated licensing and civil enforcement is the logical fix.

The enforcement picture exposed in the hearing is genuinely damning. Of approximately 1,200 criminal pedicab summonses issued in 2025, around 34-35% were deemed legally insufficient, 25% dismissed in the interest of justice, 20% not pursued, 9% never prosecuted, and only 9% resulted in any sanction. DCWP received 335 overcharging complaints since 2022 and achieved zero resolutions, largely because tourists do not follow up. The problem is structural: criminal summonses require discovery compliance that NYPD cannot realistically provide for what are essentially regulatory infractions, charges are routinely dismissed, confiscated bikes are returned within days, and there is no data-sharing between NYPD enforcement actions and the licensing agency that could revoke operating authority. The Midtown Community Justice Center's director put it plainly: the cases actually appearing in court are technical violations like missing tail lights, while the consumer fraud and overcharging that everyone cares about is not being addressed at all. Multiple witnesses and Council Members observed that the industry now has the character of a gold rush, with unlicensed operators openly bragging about charging tourists whatever they can get away with. The hearing closed without votes, but with clear Council intent to advance the package.
