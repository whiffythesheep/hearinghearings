Waste Valorization and the Circular Economy

Committee on Sanitation and Solid Waste Management

Chair: Justin E. Sanchez
September 15, 2026 · 2hrs 9m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Sanitation and Solid Waste Management held an oversight hearing on waste valorization and the circular economy, alongside consideration of two bills and two resolutions. The substantive testimony came primarily from DSNY Commissioner Gregory Anderson and his team, with public witnesses representing environmental advocacy groups, canners, and homeowners. The hearing covered a wide range of topics including textile recycling, composting infrastructure, co-digestion of organics at wastewater facilities, nightlife district street cleaning, illegal dumping, and the bottle bill.

Commissioner Anderson's testimony was notable for its candor on several fronts. He acknowledged that the food donation portal DSNY operates has been essentially useless, moving only around five tons of food per year against the 40,000 tons moved annually by partner organizations like City Harvest, and suggested the City's resources would be better directed toward supporting those nonprofits' transportation and real estate constraints. On textiles, he was measured about the prospects for curbside collection, citing two failed pilots, while flagging the sector as a priority for the forthcoming Solid Waste Management Plan. On emerging waste streams, he called out lithium batteries as a growing hazard causing truck fires and made a pointed call for extended producer responsibility legislation in Albany, estimating it could deliver over $100 million annually to New York City's recycling program alone. On alternative waste-processing technologies such as pyrolysis, he pushed back against what he called the "emotional" framing that has historically blocked scientific assessment of promising options.

The co-digestion discussion was the most technically detailed portion of the hearing, with DEP's Jane Godwani explaining the Newtown Creek model, where Waste Management pre-processes food waste into pumpable bio-slurry at no cost to the City, which is then co-digested with sewage sludge and converted to renewable natural gas distributed through National Grid's grid under the federal Renewable Fuel Standard. The Hunts Point wastewater facility is undergoing a parallel buildout, with new anaerobic digesters expected online in 2026 and potential capacity of up to 400 tons of food waste per day, subject to a nitrogen management assessment. The committee pressed hard on environmental justice implications for the South Bronx, including truck traffic, odor, and cumulative air quality impacts, with CM Louis noting the district's already high asthma rates. DEP flagged interest in barge transport as a lower-impact alternative.

Public testimony was dominated by two threads. The first was a passionate and well-evidenced case for expanding the bottle bill, presented by Ryan Castelli of Can Can and Cecilia Ellis of NYPIRG, alongside direct testimony from canners Petra and Jose Marin. Canners currently earn roughly five dollars an hour; the deposit has not increased in forty years; and a ten-cent deposit, expanded container coverage, and increased handling fees are estimated to save New York City between $35 million and $80 million annually while dramatically improving return rates and supporting marginalized workers. The resolution before the committee, Res 0362-2026, calls on Albany to act, and the testimony made clear that the committee should be loudly reinforcing that call. The second public thread concerned containerization, with a homeowner testifying about the practical and financial absurdity of being ticketed for setting out bags after her bins were stolen, and an advocate calling for containerization pilots that include recycling and composting infrastructure alongside trash, arguing that trash-only containerization risks worsening diversion rates.

The NRDC flagged the most consequential broader issue: the Draft Solid Waste Management Plan currently circulating is, in their assessment, insufficiently ambitious and too vague to serve as a meaningful planning document, and the committee should prioritize engaging with and strengthening it before finalization. Given that the SWMP is supposed to guide resource recovery for the next decade, this is not a minor concern.
