Meeting overview

The New York City Council Committee on Workforce Development held an oversight hearing on the Office of Community Hiring (OCH), examining whether the program is actually connecting low-income New Yorkers to jobs on City contracts, whether its data reporting meets legal requirements, and whether it has the staff, systems and accountability tools to scale.

Chair Julie Won opened with a sharp framing of the program's mixed record. The state legislation authorizing community hiring passed in 2023, the City's rules took effect in January 2025, and the administration had projected 36,000 jobs annually at full implementation. The most recent quarterly report covered 179 contracts worth roughly $3 billion — real growth from 60 contracts worth $1 billion — but the substance behind those numbers was thin. Of 83 construction contracts, only eight reported any progress, and six of those eight were below the 30% community hiring goal. All five contracts with apprenticeship goals reported zero apprentice hours from community hires. Across 94 service contracts with combined goals of 3,150 income-based hires, 53 showed no progress data at all. And critically, nowhere in the published reports did OCH state how many people had actually been hired — a legal requirement under the authorizing legislation.

Douglas Lipari, Executive Director of NYC Talent, and G. Lee, Executive Director of the Office of Community Hiring, testified for the administration. Their headline figure was approximately 1,200 community hires to date across all contracts, with roughly 1,100 income-based and 70 residence-based. They emphasized the program's novelty — the first of its kind nationally to cover not just construction but human services, professional services and standard services — and pointed to 528 registered contracts worth $13.3 billion and 362 procurements released worth $18.4 billion as evidence of real momentum. They also noted that 23.75% of hours on construction contracts had been performed by community hires, against a 30% goal, which is at least in the right ballpark. The administration's consistent defense was that the program is early-stage, contracts are multi-year, and vendors' hiring needs don't all materialise on day one.

Council Members pushed hard on the gap between those reassuring framing points and the actual published data. CM Epstein raised the absence of outreach to well-established settlement houses and employment networks in his district — organizations like Henry Street Settlement and the Lower East Side Employment Network that are doing exactly the kind of work OCH needs — and noted that a veteran in his district had been job-searching for six months without knowing community hiring existed. CM Phil Wong asked OCH to come do a job fair in his district, made the point that he had never heard of OCH before the hearing, and pressed repeatedly for a concrete count of jobs available across the 528 contracts, receiving a non-committal answer about following up in two weeks. The confusion between the City's new Lafayette Street jobs center (focused on City employment) and the community hiring program (which covers non-City vendor jobs) illustrated a broader communication problem: the administration's various workforce initiatives are not well-integrated in public messaging or operations.

Several specific weaknesses emerged beyond the headline data gaps. OCH's referral source network includes only 28 community-based organizations, notably absent many major settlement houses and neighborhood employment programs. The office's outreach materials are English-only. OCH has a 16-position budget allocation but only eight staff in its core team, with three vacancies having no active job postings and no defined titles. The $388,200 contractual services budget is currently supporting a single consultant firm rather than any technology contracts. The penalty framework — $2,500 plus $1,000 per week for noncompliance — has never been used, and no vendor has received a notice of noncompliance. The income threshold for community hires was set at 300% of the federal poverty level rather than 150%, expanding the eligible pool upward but also diluting the program's focus on the lowest-income New Yorkers. Chair Won noted the uncomfortable parallel to the failed 2019 Hire NYC program, where 80% of job referrals sat marked pending for years because employers never reported outcomes — a failure mode OCH is at risk of repeating given the current state of its data collection.

  • 528 registered contracts with community hiring goals as of the hearing date, valued at $13.3 billion.
  • 179 registered contracts with community hiring goals as of the most recent quarterly report (covering through June 30, 2026), valued at approximately $3 billion.
  • 362 procurements with community hiring goals released to date, valued at $18.4 billion.
  • Approximately 1,200 community hires employed under the program to date.
  • Approximately 1,100 of the 1,200 hires are income-based community hires.
  • Approximately 70 of the 1,200 hires are residence-based community hires.
  • 23.75% of hours worked on construction contracts have been performed by community hires, against a 30% goal.
  • 0% of hours worked on apprenticeship-goal contracts have been performed by community hire apprentices, against a 9% goal.
  • 83 construction contracts carry community hiring goals; only 8 reported any progress; 6 of those 8 were below the 30% goal.
  • 94 service contracts carry combined income-based community hiring goals totaling approximately 3,150 hires; 53 of the 94 showed no progress data.
  • One Department of Social Services PACE contract in Queens had a goal of 31 hires and showed 1,229% progress, implying several hundred actual hires against that single contract.
  • Program originally projected to generate 36,000 community hire jobs annually at full implementation.
  • City contracts subject to community hiring are estimated to represent approximately 90% of all services contract value procured annually by mayoral agencies.
  • OCH FY2027 budget: $1.7 million with 16 allocated positions; $388,200 set aside for contractual services.
  • OCH core team: 8 staff, plus 1 AmeriCorps Civic Corps member and 1 intern; 3 current vacancies with no active postings.
  • 2 additional community hiring headcount positions embedded at the Department of Small Business Services.
  • Income threshold for community hires: up to 300% of the federal poverty level (approximately $47,000-$48,000 for a single-person household).
  • 28 community-based organizations currently authorized as referral sources; 81 referral source organizations in total including labor unions and Workforce1 centers.
  • OCH held approximately 200 meetings with City agency procurement staff and trained over 115 agency staff across 27 agencies during rollout.
  • September 30, 2026 community hiring job fair: 20-25 employer participants, 388 job seekers, 16 conditional job offers made on the spot.
  • Borough breakdown of human services contract community hires: 27.5% Bronx, 36.5% Brooklyn, 12% Manhattan, 22% Queens, 1.5% Staten Island.
  • November 2025 agreement with building trades to fill 700 pre-apprenticeship positions for NYCHA residents and low-income community members.
  • City goal: connect 30,000 New Yorkers to apprenticeships by 2030.
  • Penalty for vendor noncompliance: $2,500 base plus $1,000 per week for failure to take corrective action; no penalties assessed to date.
▸ Action Points
  • OCH to provide the Committee with the total number of hours worked by community hires on construction contracts and the percentage of total hours those represent.
  • OCH to provide the Committee within two weeks with a breakdown of available jobs across all 528 registered contracts, including contract types, industries and skill requirements.
  • OCH to share with Council Members the full list of 28 authorized community-based referral source organizations.
  • OCH to engage Council Member offices as outreach channels, including using Council Member email lists and communications networks to publicize community hiring opportunities.
  • OCH to reach out to Council Member Epstein's office regarding Lower East Side employment organizations not currently in the referral source network, including Henry Street Settlement, Grand Street Settlement and University Settlement.
  • OCH to follow up with Council Member Wong on hosting a community hiring job fair in his district.
  • OCH to provide the Committee with the specific penalty calculation formula and contract language governing noncompliance.
  • OCH to provide the Committee with data on how construction contracts covered by project labor agreements are being tracked separately for community hiring compliance.
  • OCH to share available demographic and compensation data on community hires directly with Chair Won, even ahead of formal quarterly report publication.
  • OCH to provide the Committee with an update on how many of the 700 pre-apprenticeship positions announced in November 2025 have been filled.
  • OCH to confirm and clarify the number of budgeted, filled and vacant positions relative to the 16-position FY2027 allocation, and report back to the Committee.
  • OCH to develop and publish demographic and compensation data in future quarterly reports as required by the City Charter, and to provide a timeline for when this will be included.
  • OCH to ensure future quarterly reports include a clear total count of community hires, as legally required.
  • OCH to expand outreach materials beyond English to at least five languages, rather than relying solely on referral source partners for language access.
  • OCH to provide the Committee with details on the JK Consultant engagement, including scope of work and contract value.
  • OCH Executive Director Lee to report back at the next quarterly cycle on progress toward finalizing digital data collection systems, expanding the referral source network and broadening vendor outreach.

Community hiring exemplifies Talent's efforts to design and launch interagency initiatives to deliver a better workforce system. Community hiring is a first-of-its-kind program in the nation that aims to embed equity and opportunity into City contracting.

New York City's local government has many City agencies that provide a wide range of services for New Yorkers, from designing parks and playgrounds to providing health care and child care, to roads and bridges. While some of this work is done by City employees, the City also contracts with vendors, which include businesses and nonprofits, to deliver billions of dollars in services every year. Community hiring allows the City to leverage its purchasing power by setting workforce goals on these contracts for vendors to hire low income individuals and residents of low income communities, including NYCHA housing.

Community hiring was authorized by state legislation in 2023 and Talent was tasked with transforming the framework established by the law into an operational program that has a tangible impact on New Yorkers. While there are similar programs in other cities and states that are generally limited to the construction industry, the City's program goes further and applies to a broad range of contracts including construction, human services like health, standard services like cleaning and moving and professional services like technology and engineering. OCH manages the rollout and implementation of the program, supports City agencies and vendors and reports on progress toward community hiring goals.

We are proud to share that as a result of these efforts, 528 contracts have community hiring goals, which represents $13.3 billion in contract value.
