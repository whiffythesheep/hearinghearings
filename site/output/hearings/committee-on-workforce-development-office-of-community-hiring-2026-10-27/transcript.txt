Good afternoon and welcome to today's oversight hearing of the Committee on Workforce Development. My name is Julie Won and I have the privilege of chairing this Committee. Before we begin I would like to recognize my colleagues who have joined us: CM Hanif and CM Epstein.

Today we are looking at the Office of Community Hiring. The idea behind community hiring is simple. The City spends billions of dollars each year on contracts, and the companies or nonprofits that get awarded those contracts should be hiring New Yorkers who need the work. In 2023 Albany gave the City the power to set hiring goals on City contracts for low income New Yorkers, NYCHA residents and people living in our highest poverty neighborhoods. The City's own rules took effect in January 2025. When the program launched, the administration projected 36,000 jobs a year once it was fully up and running. New York City Talent told this Committee that more than 200 procurements were already out on the street and that the program could reach upwards of $20 billion in City contracts each year.

But then we started looking at the Office of Community Hiring's own reports. OCH's latest quarterly report was posted just recently and it shows 179 contracts worth about $3 billion carrying a community hiring goal, up from 60 contracts worth about $1 billion. That is real growth. But unfortunately the progress has not kept up. Of the 83 construction contracts, only eight reported any progress and six of these eight were below the 30% community hiring goal.

On all five contracts with an apprenticeship goal that reported progress, the share of hours worked by apprentices from these communities was zero. For service contracts, 94 contracts now carry community hiring goals that add up to no progress reported at all. And some of the numbers that are reported do not add up. One Department of Social Services workforce development program in Queens has a goal of 31 hires but OCH's report shows it at more than 1,200% of that goal. Either something remarkable is happening or OCH is counting something other than new hires. And nowhere in these reports does the City say how many people have actually been hired. The law requires these reports to show how many people were hired, what they are paid and who they are, and that information is not in there.

So today I want to understand what is behind these numbers. Where nothing has been reported, has the work already started and vendors just are not reporting? Or is it that OCH does not have the staff or the systems to follow up? New York City Talent told us that it has 37 staff in total.

I want to know how many of them are working on community hiring and whether that is enough to keep up with a program that has nearly tripled in size in a single quarter. The City expected those contracts to be placed by 2025. They still do not appear in any of the reports.

This is not the City's first try at this. Back in 2019, under Hire in New York City, about 80% of job referrals were marked as pending for years because employers never told the City whether anyone was hired. We cannot afford to repeat that.

NYC Talent's mission is to ensure that the City's workforce system connects New Yorkers to good jobs and helps employers access local talent. Our public workforce ecosystem includes more than 120 programs administered by nearly 30 City agencies, including the Department of Small Business Services, the Human Resources Administration, the Department of Youth and Community Development, the City University of New York and New York City Public Schools. While each agency focuses on its own core mission, Talent works across these partners to align the City's workforce strategy with job seeker needs, employer demand and a changing economy.

Community hiring exemplifies Talent's efforts to design and launch interagency initiatives to deliver a better workforce system. Community hiring is a first-of-its-kind program in the nation that aims to embed equity and opportunity into City contracting.

New York City's local government has many City agencies that provide a wide range of services for New Yorkers, from designing parks and playgrounds to providing health care and child care, to roads and bridges. While some of this work is done by City employees, the City also contracts with vendors, which include businesses and nonprofits, to deliver billions of dollars in services every year. Community hiring allows the City to leverage its purchasing power by setting workforce goals on these contracts for vendors to hire low income individuals and residents of low income communities, including NYCHA housing.

Community hiring was authorized by state legislation in 2023 and Talent was tasked with transforming the framework established by the law into an operational program that has a tangible impact on New Yorkers. While there are similar programs in other cities and states that are generally limited to the construction industry, the City's program goes further and applies to a broad range of contracts including construction, human services like health, standard services like cleaning and moving and professional services like technology and engineering. OCH manages the rollout and implementation of the program, supports City agencies and vendors and reports on progress toward community hiring goals.

We are proud to share that as a result of these efforts, 528 contracts have community hiring goals, which represents $13.3 billion in contract value. Despite most of these contracts being recently registered and early on in their contract term, we already have approximately 1,200 community hires employed by vendors to date.

I want to take this opportunity to walk you through the work that has made these accomplishments possible. After the state legislation that authorized community hiring was signed by the

(00:07:47)

Governor in November 2023, we were required to complete the City's formal rulemaking process to establish the operational framework for the program. This included determining details about what type of contracts are covered, how City agencies set goals and what vendors need to do to comply with goals. OCH worked collaboratively while developing these rules so they would be informed by the perspectives of the many stakeholders impacted by community hiring. OCH heard from 120 unique external partners through one-on-one meetings, information sessions, roundtables and panel discussions. These groups included organized labor, elected officials, workforce development organizations, industry groups, pre-apprenticeship programs, human service providers and community organizations. OCH also engaged with 47 different City agencies and offices through one-on-one meetings and biweekly agency working groups. After this process, the community hiring rules went into effect January 23, 2025.

Since community hiring is tied to City contracting, our major priority in 2025 was ensuring that City agency staff were aware, trained and prepared to set goals on their agency's applicable contracts. We reviewed contract information from Passport, monitored procurement being released by agencies and held approximately 200 meetings with agency procurement staff to discuss opportunities to set goals. We held training sessions that were attended by over 115 City agency staff representing 27 agencies, developed an agency-specific FAQ document and created standard contract language. After a phased rollout, by September 2025 we had supported all mayoral agencies to ensure they were prepared to implement community hiring.

We also engaged City vendors so that they had the information they needed to understand, comply with and benefit from community hiring. These efforts included publishing a vendor-specific document on our website, attending community and procurement events to conduct vendor outreach and presenting during pre-solicitation conferences to provide guidance to vendors interested in responding to solicitations for procurements with goals. To date we have presented at 77 pre-solicitation conferences.

To help vendors fill their workforce needs and meet their community hiring goals, we established a network of referral sources, which are entities authorized by our office to connect vendors with qualified job seekers in search of opportunities. We intentionally designed our referral source network to drive more employers and more jobs to the public workforce system. Currently our network includes 81 organizations including labor unions, SBS's Workforce1 Career Centers, Business Link and dozens of community-based organizations that have contracts with agencies like DYCD for job placement services.

Referral sources conduct outreach to their communities, provide free job placement and recruitment services, determine job seekers are eligible as community hires and help ensure that candidates meet the vendor's employment needs. Accountability is embedded into the design of community hiring as we are required to publish quarterly reports on contracts subject to community hiring goals. These reports include aggregate and vendor-specific progress towards goals and information about placements and demographics of community hires. Regular reporting will not only increase transparency but will also provide a long-term opportunity for the City to continuously improve the program by identifying opportunities for targeted outreach or training programs that could be developed to better align the skills of job seekers with the needs of City vendors.

While we are still building some of the necessary digital infrastructure, we have published nine quarterly reports to date on our website at nyc.gov/community-hiring. Under the leadership of Mayor Mamdani and Deputy Mayor for Economic Justice Charlie Sue, we are committed to strengthening the workforce system as essential public infrastructure to connect New Yorkers to opportunity. Community hiring is an important tool that strategically leverages City contracts to drive employers and jobs to the public workforce system, while also improving interconnectivity across City agencies, vendors and programs so that job seekers and businesses mutually benefit.

I know firsthand from working many years at the Mayor's Office of Contract Services that City procurement is an extremely complicated process that offers immense opportunity. Embedding community into this process, developing multiple digital systems for data tracking and analysis, strengthening collaborations with our partners and organized labor through project labor agreements and preparing numerous disparate stakeholders for program implementation has been an enormous effort. I am incredibly proud of the progress we have made to date but we know we still have a lot of work to do. We look forward to answering your questions and discussing how we can work together to realize the full vision of community hiring and connect more New Yorkers to quality employment opportunities. Thank you and we are happy to take your questions.
