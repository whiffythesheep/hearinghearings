Work Order Practices at NYCHA and PACT

Committee on Public Housing

Chair: Chris Banks
September 22, 2026 · 2hrs 55m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Public Housing held an oversight hearing on work order practices at NYCHA Section 9 developments and PACT-converted properties. The core question: does the system that residents use to get repairs actually work? The answer, based on testimony from residents, community advocates, NYCHA officials and PACT property managers, is: sometimes, for some people, with a great deal of variation and opacity.

Pre-panel testimony from residents and advocates painted a grim picture. A dialysis patient living without kitchen cabinets and a functioning sink for months; a grandmother whose husband was hospitalized with asthma attacks caused by plaster dust left behind by NYCHA workers; a Stuyvesant Gardens resident describing windows that fall inward when opened, asbestos encapsulated without residents' knowledge, apartments permanently altered for wheelchair accessibility without showing tenants the new floor plans, and renovation costs reportedly of $380,000 per unit that residents say bear no relationship to the actual quality of work delivered. A community organizer testified that constituent service work in his district is effectively dominated by NYCHA work order follow-up, and that his mother's two-month wait for kitchen cabinets was resolved in two days after Council Member intervention. Leila Glasgow testified that at Fulton Houses and Elliot Chelsea, slated for demolition, NYCHA has apparently abandoned routine maintenance while simultaneously using repair requests as opportunities to pressure residents to accept relocation. One bright spot was testimony from a resident leader at East Chester Gardens, where PACT manager Wave Crest has implemented a digital platform called Property Meld, achieving a 98% completion rate, 24-hour median response time and 4.1-star resident satisfaction ratings, and has created a joint tracking system with the tenant association. The contrast with Section 9 performance was stark and was not lost on the committee.

NYCHA's Executive Vice President for Property Management Operations Daniel Green and Chief Real Estate Officer Heather Back presented the agency's case. The headline statistics are: 606,583 open work orders as of September 21, 2026, broken into roughly 17,000 maintenance, 311,000 skilled trades, 8,000 other crafts and 221,000 vendor tickets. About 132,000 work orders have been open for more than a year, with paint tickets constituting the largest category. The average wait for a carpentry appointment is currently seven months. NYCHA is completing emergency work orders in an average of 11 hours and non-emergency orders in 55.6 days, though the skilled trades and vendor backlog skews this significantly. Green argued, with some credibility, that the backlog is partly an artifact of NYCHA now doing things it never did before: inspecting 100% of apartments annually, applying strict closure rules that require physical verification, and proactively creating work orders rather than waiting for resident complaints. HUD NSPIRE inspection pass rates have risen from 15% in 2022-23 to 89% in 2026, and the average score has risen from 32.5 to 79.5. Council Member Encarnacion was openly skeptical of these figures given what her office sees on the ground daily, and said so bluntly. Chair Banks pressed Green on whether the system is fundamentally broken, pointing out that resident intervention only happened because the Council office interceded, and that a seven-month wait for cabinets is not a defensible service standard. Green pushed back, arguing that NYCHA is completing more repairs than ever before, that ticket closure rules are now genuinely rigorous, and that the 2013-2014 episode of rapid backlog reduction was almost certainly driven by premature or fraudulent closures rather than real repairs. The $78 billion capital needs assessment remains the underlying structural reality that no amount of operational reform can resolve without massive new investment.

On the PACT side, Back presented a rosier picture, noting that only 409 work orders are currently open across the 82,000 created in the PACT portfolio over six years, with typical resolution within one day in the five monitored pillar areas. However, the committee correctly noted that PACT data is far less granular than Section 9 data, is self-reported by private managers using different software systems with no standardization requirement, and that on-time resolution rates have declined from 90.75% in 2023 to 74.5% in the first part of 2025 as the portfolio has grown. Banks asked pointed questions about what happens to open work orders when a development converts from Section 9 to PACT, whether NYCHA tracks falsification at PACT sites (answer: no), and whether the historically poor transparency of NYCHA's work order data justifies the agency being the only major landlord in New York City that effectively inspects itself. NYCHA did not object to greater transparency or external inspection in principle, but offered no commitments to change practice.

▸ Action Points
  • NYCHA to provide committee with open work order data broken down by year going back to 2019, including Section 9 unit counts and work order counts for 2022 and 2023.
  • NYCHA to provide committee with a breakdown of work orders open for more than two years.
  • NYCHA to provide the cost estimate and headcount required to reduce the Section 9 work order backlog from current levels to 200,000 and maintain it there over a five-year period.
  • NYCHA to provide data on work order closures made without completed repair, separately from closures with confirmed repair, and to consider publishing this data publicly.
  • NYCHA to provide data on the number of employees disciplined or terminated for falsifying work order entries since 2020.
  • NYCHA to provide data on referrals to the DOI Inspector General for suspected work order falsification since 2020 and the number substantiated.
  • NYCHA to provide data on resident complaints of falsified or improperly closed work orders in fiscal year 2025 and the number investigated.
  • NYCHA to provide housing court data for fiscal years 2022, 2023 and 2024 on HPD violations associated with tenant-initiated cases, violations corrected after court filing, and violations requiring a court order or stipulation.
  • NYCHA to provide data on quality control inspections of closed work orders for fiscal year 2025, including the share found to be inaccurately reported.
  • NYCHA to provide skilled trades headcount breakdown by trade and clarify the budget implications of filling vacancies to reduce the backlog.
  • NYCHA to consider extending publicly available work order data on its website from one year to a full historical series from 2019 to present.
  • NYCHA to investigate and report back on whether it is feasible to publish closed-without-repair work order data quarterly.
  • NYCHA leadership to consider and respond to Chair Banks' proposal that NYCHA apartments be subject to independent external inspection and that findings be published.
  • NYCHA to report to committee on whether any conversations have occurred with HPD about creating a streamlined process allowing tenants to obtain an inspection and a repair without two separate trips to housing court.
  • NYCHA to provide data on OSC intake volume from PACT properties going back to the start of the program, in addition to the 39 tickets reported for the last quarter.
  • NYCHA to provide data comparing completion rates for work orders submitted through the OSC versus those submitted through other channels.
  • NYCHA to confirm the launch timeline for the Critical Repairs Initiative and report back to committee with an estimate of the number of units likely to qualify.
  • NYCHA to confirm when elevator door parts funding will be received and provide a projected repair schedule to committee.
  • NYCHA to report back on whether PACT partners can be required to report suspected work order falsification to NYCHA, and whether the control agreements should be amended accordingly.
  • NYCHA to provide a breakdown of work order data by development to assist committee in assessing whether PACT transitions are affecting maintenance performance at remaining Section 9 sites.
