Good morning. I am CM Selvena Brooks-Powers, Chair of the Committee on Criminal Justice. I want to welcome everyone to today's hearing on the organizational strategy of the Department of Probation.

The Department of Probation plays an important role in New York City's criminal legal system. Its responsibilities extend beyond supervising individuals in probation to include court-related investigations, juvenile intake and adjustment, and connecting people under supervision with services that support their stability and successful completion of probation. The Department's organizational approach affects how these responsibilities are carried out: how offices interact with the people they supervise, what the outcomes are for people under Department supervision, and how effectively young people and families navigate the court system.

In recent years, the Department has faced significant challenges related to staffing shortages, employee retention, caseload management, and the direction of its supervision policies. As of the Department's reported vacancy rate, approximately 23%, or 255 vacancies, 210 of which were probation offices. The elimination of court liaison officer positions under the previous administration raises concerns about coordination with Family Court and the ability to identify opportunities for adjustment before young people enter formal court proceedings.

The return of court liaison functions, along with changes to pay for assignments and staff requirement efforts, makes it important to examine how the Department is organized today and how these decisions affect the agency's work. The Department has been under new leadership since Commissioner Goodwin's appointment. Today's hearing is an opportunity to hear directly from the Department about the changes that leadership has brought, what remains in development, and how those changes are impacting probation officers and those under their supervision.

As we examine the Department's organizational strategy, we must consider both the conditions facing probation officers and the experiences of the people under their supervision. Effective probation requires trained and supported staff, manageable and appropriately structured caseloads, coordination with providers, and access to services that address the needs contributing to justice system involvement. Today, the Committee will examine the approaches to assigning and supervising cases and the role of community-based programs in supporting successful outcomes. We will also consider how the Department evaluates program effectiveness and uses available data to guide decisions about services and resources.

In addition to today's topic, the Committee will be hearing Introduction Number 11 or 4, sponsored by myself, in relation to providing youth accused of crimes with information about the criminal legal process. This bill would require the Department of Probation to provide young people accused of crimes with notices explaining the criminal legal process.
