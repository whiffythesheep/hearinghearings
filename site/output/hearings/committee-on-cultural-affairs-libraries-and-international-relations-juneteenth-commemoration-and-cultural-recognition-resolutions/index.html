Juneteenth Commemoration and Cultural Recognition Resolutions

Committee on Cultural Affairs, Libraries and International Relations

Chair: Nantasha M. Williams
May 13, 2026 · 2hrs 3m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Cultural Affairs, Libraries and International Relations held an oversight hearing on commemorating Juneteenth and honoring burial sites of formerly enslaved African Americans, alongside consideration of Int 0220-2026 and several resolutions. The hearing was substantive and at times genuinely moving, covering difficult historical ground with a panel of archaeologists and scholars who clearly knew far more about the subject than the city agency nominally responsible for acting on the legislation.

Int 0220-2026, sponsored by CM Hanks and CM Rita Joseph, would require the Department of Cultural Affairs to maintain a list of known burial sites of formerly enslaved African Americans, develop a plan to locate unmarked sites, notify elected officials when sites are found in their districts, create an educational campaign and build an interactive public map. The expert panel -- archaeologists and scholars from CUNY, Johns Hopkins and AKARF -- broadly supported the Bill while raising pointed concerns. The most consequential of these was that the Department of Cultural Affairs is the wrong agency: the Landmarks Preservation Commission already maintains archaeological site inventories and has the regulatory expertise. Experts also pushed the Council to think more expansively than enslaved-persons burial sites specifically, since segregation of Black burial grounds continued well into the twentieth century and many significant sites post-date formal slavery. They further noted that existing law -- both city and state -- provides essentially no protection for known burial sites on private land until human remains have already been disturbed, and called on the Council to pursue legislation with actual teeth rather than documentation alone.

The Department of Cultural Affairs testimony was, diplomatically speaking, underwhelming. The Deputy Commissioner confirmed the agency had not been sent questions in advance, had not thought through whether the Bill should authorise contracts and grants, had no update on the status of the Freedom Trail Task Force created under prior Juneteenth legislation, and acknowledged that Juneteenth programming and burial site recognition are not currently connected within any coherent commemorative framework. Chair Williams noted, drily, that this was the second hearing at which the agency appeared unprepared, and committed to sharing questions in advance in future. The agency's core defence -- that it is primarily a funder, not a heritage manager, and that this work needs more deliberate inter-agency planning -- was reasonable as far as it went, but not quite adequate as a substitute for having thought about it before arriving.

CM Banks offered a detailed account of the New Lots Library in East New York, which is being rebuilt on and around the East New York African Burial Ground, with design elements intended to honour the site's history. The Brooklyn Public Library and its design team testified about the project, which represents one of the more considered examples of how a civic institution can confront this history in practice rather than in principle. Public Advocate Jumaane Williams testified in support of the Bill and connected it explicitly to the national context of efforts to suppress Black history education, describing the removal of Black history from curricula as a deliberate political objective rather than an incidental one. Chair Williams used her closing questions to link the hearing to the Council's broader reparations and truth and reconciliation work, including a current open grant application from the Commission on Racial Equity.

The resolutions on the agenda -- celebrating the Feast of San Gennaro, declaring Kappa Alpha Psi Day, Willie Colon Day and Maria Antonia Cay Day -- received brief introductory remarks but were not the focus of substantive testimony or debate.

  • Seneca Village was destroyed in one of the earliest uses of eminent domain to acquire private land in New York City, to create Central Park.
  • Dr. Meade's doctoral research documented more than 500 current and former cemeteries across the five boroughs, more than half of which were redeveloped with or without removal of human remains.
  • Documented segregated cemeteries used exclusively for free and enslaved people of African descent were the only cemetery category in Dr. Meade's research for which all sites were redeveloped and none were preserved.
  • New Yorkers of African descent represented between one tenth and one third of the City's population over time and experienced higher mortality rates than the general population.
  • Colonial authorities mandated segregation of cemeteries in 1697 and passed laws restricting Black funerals throughout the eighteenth century.
  • New York State's Unmarked Burial Site Protection Act went into effect in 2023 but applies only after remains have already been encountered during subsurface activity.
  • The federal Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act protecting Indigenous burial grounds was passed in 1990; no comparable federal legislation exists for Black burial grounds.
  • A federal grant of approximately $3 million was reportedly set aside through the National Park Service for states to distribute to Black cemetery preservation efforts, though its disbursement is uncertain.
  • The Flatbush African Burial Ground was transferred from Parks in 2022 following community advocacy for its preservation as open space.
  • The Department of Cultural Affairs announced $10 million in new budget funding to be directed toward grant opportunities for cultural organisations.
  • The Society of Black Archaeologists was founded in 2011 or 2012 and now has approximately 200 members.
  • Institute for the Exploration of Seneca Village History conducted archaeological excavations in Seneca Village in summer 2011 following more than a decade of prior documentary research.
