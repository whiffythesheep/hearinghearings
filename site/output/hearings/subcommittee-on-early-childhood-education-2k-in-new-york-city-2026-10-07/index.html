Chair: Jennifer Gutiérrez
October 7, 2026 · 3hrs 1m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Subcommittee on Early Childhood Education, chaired by Jennifer Gutiérrez, held an oversight hearing on 2K, Mayor Mamdani's new universal early childhood education program for two-year-olds. The hearing covered the program's launch, enrollment results, funding structure, provider payment challenges, workforce pipeline, and plans for a major expansion from 2,000 seats to 12,000 seats by fall 2027. Government testimony was provided by Emily List, Executive Director of the Mayor's Office of Child Care and Early Childhood Education, and Eric Philips, Deputy Chancellor for Early Childhood Education at New York City Public Schools. Public testimony came from parent advocates, community-based organizations, provider networks, advocacy groups, and individual daycare operators.

The administration's case was straightforward: 5,700 applications for 2,000 seats proves demand; the program launched on time on September 10 with children in seats; state funding of $73 million for year one and $425 million for year two is committed; and the goal is universal coverage of all city two-year-olds by end of Mamdani's first term in 2029. Roughly 60% of seats are in home-based family child care settings via networks, with the remainder in community-based centers. No 2K seats are in public school buildings, a deliberate policy choice to honor existing toddler-care expertise in community settings. Full-day, full-year is the default model, which providers and advocates strongly endorsed as essential to working families. The five initial districts were selected based on economic need, lack of existing subsidized toddler care, and licensed provider capacity. Staten Island was excluded from year one due to insufficient provider readiness and scale challenges, but is committed for the fall 2027 expansion.

The hearing's most substantive tensions were around systemic operational failures and whether they can be fixed fast enough to support a six-fold enrollment expansion. Contracting and payment delays at launch caused real hardship for providers, with some nearly unable to open. The administration acknowledged the delays were unacceptable, cited bridge financing through an interest-free returnable grant fund as a stopgap, and pointed to 228 new hires across NYC Public Schools as the structural fix. Council members and public witnesses were skeptical that the same systems experiencing payment failures this year could handle 12,000 seats next year. Provider groups, including the Daycare Council of New York and United Neighborhood Houses, documented that 74% of providers found staffing difficult or very difficult, and that rates do not reflect the true cost of care. A reported $12,000 gap between actual program costs and reimbursement rates was cited. Pay parity between community-based educators and DOE counterparts was the single most repeated grievance across nearly every public witness, with providers describing losing qualified teachers to DOE schools over compensation differences. Several operators described choosing between making payroll and paying building costs.

Family child care network coverage was another significant structural issue. Only about 20% of family child care programs are currently in a network, yet 2K requires network affiliation for home-based providers. Chair Gutiérrez pressed hard on whether independent providers, who represent the majority of the family child care sector and are concentrated in boroughs like Brooklyn and Queens, can be included in the expansion. The administration acknowledged this is a live problem, committed to ongoing engagement, and stated that networks should not be charging providers for affiliation. The City is contracting with over 40 networks across 2K, 3K, and pre-K programs including Staten Island. Witnesses from All Our Kin noted that independent providers often cite the desire to control their own enrollment and concerns about network payment delays and contract inconsistency as reasons to remain outside the network system. The chair was direct that universal child care is not achievable without bringing these independent providers in.

Public witnesses added important dimensions around inclusion of children with disabilities, multilingual learner outreach, families experiencing housing instability, workforce development through CUNY apprenticeships, and the need for quality standards within seats, not just seat counts. Advocates for Children of New York flagged that unlike 3K and pre-K, there are no specialized program models for two-year-olds with significant disabilities, and that Early Intervention coordination alone is insufficient. CM Felder offered the meeting's only moment of genuine warmth, praising the administration's responsiveness to emails as the best he had seen since Bloomberg, which while faint praise, landed as a genuine compliment given the context.

  • 5,700 applications received for 2,000 2K seats in the initial application round.
  • 2,018 formal offers made to families on offer day.
  • 59% of families who received offers got their first-choice program.
  • 82% of families who received offers got one of their top three choices.
  • 99% of families who received offers got a program they had listed on their application.

  • $425 million committed by the Governor for year two, covering the 2027-2028 school year.
  • 2,000 seats funded in year one, expanding to 12,000 total seats in fall 2027, an increase of 10,000 seats.
  • Goal of universal coverage for all New York City two-year-olds by fall 2029.
  • Approximately 60% of 2K seats are in home-based family child care settings.
  • 4 flat reimbursement rate tiers: center-based extended day/year, center-based school day/year, family child care extended day/year, and family child care school day/year.
  • Year one 2K extended day rate approximately $29,000 per child; school day rate approximately $19,000 per child.
  • 228 new positions being hired across NYC Public Schools to support early childhood expansion, including instructional coaches, social workers, inclusion specialists, and finance and contracting staff.
  • 590 current staff in the early childhood division at NYC Public Schools as of October 4, 2026.
  • Mayor's Office of Child Care team of 12, described as fully staffed with two pending hires.
  • Over 40 family child care networks currently contracted for 2K, 3K, and pre-K across all five boroughs.
  • Only approximately 20% of all family child care programs citywide are part of a network.
  • $2.5 million invested by the Council to support CUNY early childhood apprenticeship expansion.
  • Average annual cost of full-time childcare for a two-year-old in New York City is $26,000.
  • One witness cited $36,000 per year as the cost of full-time center-based daycare in Bed-Stuy.
  • Comptroller estimate cited that a family needs to earn $334,000 per year to afford childcare for one child.
  • Citizens Committee for Children estimated 90% of New York families with young children cannot afford care without assistance.
  • Reported approximately $12,000 gap between actual program operating costs and current city reimbursement rates.
  • 87% of provider organizations surveyed by Daycare Council of New York expressed interest in participating in 2K.
  • 74% of providers described recruiting qualified staff as difficult or very difficult.
  • More than 80% of providers cited low compensation, longer hours, and competition from public schools as staffing barriers.
  • Daycare Council of New York represents nearly 140 nonprofit organizations and 42 Queens family child care network providers, collectively serving more than 13,000 children.
  • United Neighborhood Houses member organizations serve over 8,000 children in early childhood programs annually.
  • Early Starters pre-apprenticeship program with Bank Street College has graduated 33 mothers, with 19 hired by Early Starters and 5 now pursuing full credentials.
