Housing Instability Among LGBTQ+ Individuals in NYC

Committee on General Welfare | Committee on Women and Gender Equity

Chair: Crystal Hudson
June 24, 2026 · 2hrs 41m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Summary

Meeting Overview

The Committee on General Welfare and the Committee on Women and Gender Equity held a joint oversight hearing on housing instability among LGBTQ+ individuals in New York City. The hearing featured testimony from city agency officials, advocacy organizations, service providers and individuals with lived experience of LGBTQ+ homelessness. No legislation was voted on, though advocates repeatedly referenced specific budget asks and one pending bill, Int 0231, which would make anti-discrimination protections for intersex people explicit under the City Human Rights Law.

The administration was represented by the newly created Mayor's Office of LGBTQIA+ Affairs (two months old at the time of the hearing), the Department of Social Services covering both HRA and DHS, and the Department of Youth and Community Development. Agency testimony was generally competent but heavy on process descriptions and light on outcomes data, which is not surprising given the combination of a new mayoral office still in planning mode and the structural difficulty of collecting voluntary demographic data at shelter intake. The Office of LGBTQ+ Affairs Director was candid about being in a phased planning process, still negotiating its budget, and not yet having active community outreach campaigns — an honest but not especially reassuring posture given the urgency described elsewhere in the hearing. DHS provided incident tracking data on discrimination and harassment in shelters, though tracking remains fragmented across the ombudsman system, 311, provider-level complaints and 911 calls, which are not currently consolidated.

DYCD reported that 38% of youth entering its programs identify as LGBTQ+, representing serious overrepresentation relative to the general population. However, data limitations pervade the system: youth are not required to disclose sexual orientation or gender identity, the 40,000 annual figure includes outreach contacts and duplicates, and long-term housing outcome data beyond one year is not readily available. The hearing surfaced a significant structural problem in the youth homelessness system: the gap between the city's reimbursement rate of $50,000 per bed per year and the actual cost of $80,000, which is causing providers to decline new bed allocations even when they are offered. Ali Forney Center testified that 400 youth are currently on its waitlist for a crisis bed, up from 300 the prior year, and that the waitlist fell below 100 when housing vouchers were available.

Public testimony was often the most substantive part of the hearing. Advocates from Ali Forney, SAGE, Point Source Youth, Legal Aid Society, Manhattan Legal Services, New York Trans Juggle and others described specific systemic failures: the IDNYC caretaker list excluding DHS, leaving shelter clients unable to obtain ID to access housing programs; HASA clients losing apartments because nobody informed them the rent ceiling was too low; trans and queer migrants facing shelter violence and police interaction with no interpretation access; and a foster care to homelessness pipeline for LGBTQ+ youth with no systematic transition planning. SAGE flagged a 250% increase in adults 55 and older in shelter between 2004 and 2017, with LGBTQ+ seniors at particular risk given lifetime employment and housing discrimination. The Point Source Youth testimony was the most emotionally direct, presenting the Targeted Housing Assistance Program's cash assistance model, which a Johns Hopkins evaluation found resulted in 98% of participants having no shelter contact in six months at an average cost of $3,700 per person.

The hearing reflects a genuine and well-documented policy problem without a clear near-term resolution. The new mayoral office is still finding its feet; bed capacity for LGBTQ+ youth is structurally underfunded; data collection is voluntary and produces serious undercounts; and the federal threat to the Equal Access Rule adds external pressure. The council members, particularly Hudson, Nurse and Wilson, asked pointed and informed questions, pushed back appropriately on vague answers and identified the gap between stated policy and practice — most notably Hudson's challenge on whether staff or residents are moved in response to harassment complaints, and Wilson's call for post-training assessment rather than mere compliance tracking.

Numbers

  • Approximately 40% of unhoused youth in New York City identify as LGBTQ+, though they represent approximately 5-10% of the general youth population.
  • 55% of LGBTQ+ youth clients and over 67% of trans clients served by homeless youth providers became homeless primarily due to family rejection based on sexual orientation, gender identity or expression.
  • 70% of transgender shelter residents reported some form of mistreatment including harassment, assault or being kicked out in the most recent US Transgender Survey.
  • 36% of transgender people experiencing homelessness avoided shelter in the prior year due to fear of mistreatment.
  • 48% of LGBTQ+ seniors experienced discrimination when applying for housing nationally.
  • 40% of LGBTQ+ older adults live below the poverty line nationally; fewer than half own their homes compared to 65% of the general population.
  • The number of individuals aged 55 and older in the New York City shelter system increased approximately 250% between 2004 and 2017; those aged 65 and older increased more than 300%.
  • DYCD-funded runaway and homeless youth programs serve more than 40,000 young people annually, though this figure includes outreach contacts and is not deduplicated.
  • 38% of young people entered into DYCD programs identify as LGBTQ+; 35% of those in residential programs and 42% of those in drop-in centers identify as LGBTQ+.
  • As of May 2026, 700 youth identifying as LGBTQ+ were entered into DYCD residential programs; 564 were placed into case management or drop-in center services.
  • 500 of 592 youth not placed in shelter (84%) identified as LGBTQ+, according to the Local Law 79 report through 2025, with lack of bed availability cited as the reason.
  • 40 young people were referred through the streamlined referral process from January 1 through June 30, 2025.
  • Total dedicated transgender and gender nonconforming shelter beds across four boroughs: 192, distributed as 21 in the Bronx, 5 in Brooklyn, 159 in Queens and 7 in Manhattan.
  • Aces Place, the City's first transgender and gender nonconforming dedicated shelter, has 150 beds; 109 were occupied as of the week prior to the hearing.
  • Marsha's House accommodates 80 LGBTQ+ residents; there are 42 additional dedicated transgender beds across six shelters in four boroughs.
  • From July 1, 2025 through June 16, 2026, DHS and HRA recorded 92 total discrimination and harassment complaints related to LGBTQ+ and transgender status across shelter systems, including 61 gender discrimination, 24 TGNC discrimination, 5 TGNC harassment and 2 TGNC retaliation cases.
  • In FY2025, the equivalent figures were 57 gender discrimination, 7 TGNC discrimination, 5 TGNC harassment and 6 TGNC retaliation cases.
  • Under Local Law 95, only 478 LGBTQ+ individuals were listed as being served on a typical day in shelter against a daily adult shelter population of at least 50,000, representing approximately 1%.
  • 5% of HASA's approximately 36,000-client caseload (roughly 1,800 individuals) identify as transgender, gender nonconforming or non-binary; sexual orientation is not collected.
  • Ali Forney Center has 400 youth on its crisis bed waitlist as of the hearing date, up from 300 the prior year; the waitlist fell below 100 when housing vouchers were available.
  • Ali Forney Center serves over 2,000 youth per year with 212 beds and reports 25 client deaths since 2020.
  • The city pays $50,000 per runaway and homeless youth bed per year; the actual cost is $80,000 per bed.
  • Point Source Youth's Targeted Housing Assistance Program provides an average of approximately $3,700 in direct cash assistance within 48 hours; a Johns Hopkins University evaluation found 98% of participants had no shelter contact in six months.
  • The budget ask for TAP from the City Council is $4.7 million; advocates also called for a $15 million LGBTQ+ immigrant legal services fund, a $5 million baseline for the Speaker initiative fund, $10 million for the Trans Equity Initiative Fund, $10 million for support for persons involved in the sex trades and $15 million for gender-affirming care.
  • A $400,000 fund sits within DYCD for an LGBTQ+ health care program, identified as the only recurring annual funding inherited from the Unity Project model.
  • In fiscal year 2026, Marsha's House placed 28 residents into permanent long-term housing, exceeding its annual target.
  • LGBTQ+ youth who experienced homelessness or housing instability had 2 to 4 times greater odds of depression, anxiety, self-harm and suicidal ideation compared to those with stable housing.
  • Housing instability raises suicide attempt rates among LGBTQ+ youth by 3.7 times; up to 20% of homeless LGBTQ+ youth are living with HIV.
  • In a 16-month period at Borden Avenue Veterans Residence, there were 136 emergency calls, approximately one every nine hours, at an estimated cost to the City of around $20,000 per person per year.

Action Points

  • DHS to provide committee with full FY2025 and current-year data on priority-one shelter incidents involving LGBTQ+ and TGNC residents.
  • DHS to follow up with committee on breakdown of how youth enter crisis shelters by pathway (street outreach, self-referral, drop-in center referral).
  • DYCD to provide committee with a breakdown of unique versus duplicate individuals within the 40,000 annual service figure.
  • DYCD to provide committee with five-year data on LGBTQ+ youth transitioning into permanent housing where available.
  • DHS and DSS to review and report back to committee on post-training assessment mechanisms for LGBTQ+ shelter staff training, beyond current compliance tracking.
  • Mayor's Office of LGBTQ+ Affairs to keep the NYC Unity Project housing and shelter resource guide up to date and expand it across all areas of life.
  • Mayor's Office of LGBTQ+ Affairs to complete agency-wide regulatory and policy audits and follow up with committee on budget allocation details.
  • Mayor's Office of LGBTQ+ Affairs to schedule and complete planned visits to Aces Place and other dedicated TGNC shelter sites and to meet directly with housed residents.
  • Mayor's Office of LGBTQ+ Affairs to advance rights and resources report for migrant LGBTQ+ New Yorkers under Local Law 73, aiming to get ahead of the submission deadline.
  • DHS to follow up with committee on whether Staten Island transgender and gender nonconforming shelter beds are being considered as required under the Lopez settlement.
  • DHS to provide committee with data on standard actions taken in response to LGBTQ+ and TGNC discrimination complaints, and to clarify policy on whether staff or residents are relocated in response to harassment incidents.
  • DSS Commissioner to follow up with Department for the Aging on programs and partnerships supporting LGBTQ+ older adults facing housing instability.
  • DHS to provide committee with details of the forthcoming Transgender and Gender Nonconforming Advisory Group, including expected launch date and membership criteria.
  • Domestic Violence Services to submit calendar year 2025 survey data to committee under Local Law 130 when available.
  • Council members to raise conditions at Borden Avenue Veterans Residence with the DHS Commissioner and with the Veterans Committee chair.
  • Committee to follow up with interACT regarding Int 0231, which would make anti-discrimination protections for intersex people explicit under the City Human Rights Law.
  • Council to consider pressing the administration to end its lawsuit against the 2023 voucher expansion and to expand the state housing access voucher program budget allocation, per advocate requests.
