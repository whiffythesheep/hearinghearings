Habitability Checks for CityFHEPS Apartments

Committee on General Welfare

Chair: Crystal Hudson
September 22, 2026 · 3hrs 5m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The General Welfare Committee held a hearing on five bills aimed at fixing operational failures in CityFHEPS, the City's main rental assistance voucher program, which serves over 71,000 households and carries a fiscal year 2027 budget of nearly $2 billion. The bills addressed habitability inspections (Int 0098-2026), reporting of lease-up times (Int 0100-2026), application response times (Int 0101-2026), timing of rental assistance payments (Int 0641-2026), and a landlord-facing online portal (T2026-1424). DSS Commissioner Aaron Dalton testified alongside HRA Administrator Scott French and Executive Deputy Commissioner Andrea Reed. Council Members Gale Brewer (sponsor of the first three bills), Frank Morano (sponsor of the portal bill), and Carl Wilson also made statements or asked questions.

The administration's posture was a familiar one: supportive of the goals, resistant to the specific mandates, and eager to point to internal reforms already underway as a reason to hold off on legislation. On inspections, DSS described a nine-month pilot distinguishing minor from major defects, using photos, video and WhatsApp re-inspections to avoid repeat physical visits, and claimed the average inspection count per unit had fallen from 2.2 to 1.7. On application processing, DSS reported a reduction from 28 to 24.8 days following a reorganization that cut the review from three touches to two. On the landlord portal, DSS acknowledged that landlords currently must navigate at least two separate systems and cannot yet see inspection status, paperwork status and payments in one place, but argued the January 2027 deadline in Morano's bill was unworkable without committing to an alternative date. On payment timing, DSS defended its current practice of issuing City payments on the 26th of the prior month, while noting it cannot control the state's two-tranche shelter allowance payments, which can leave landlords waiting for the full amount until mid-month.

The hearing surfaced some genuinely useful data and some notable gaps. Fifty percent of HRA-conducted inspections fail on the first visit, with water temperature, electrical outlets and missing apartment numbers among the top causes. The average time from shopping letter issuance to move-in was 244 days in 2024, 221 in 2025, and 236 year-to-date in 2026, a worrying reversal. DSS does not collect data on re-inspection wait times, provider-conducted inspection failure rates, how many households have an apartment ready but are stuck in administrative processing, or the additional shelter costs attributable to those delays. The administration acknowledged it recently had to reverse a newly introduced rent reasonableness policy for rent-regulated units after it unexpectedly slowed move-ins, a quiet admission that a well-intentioned internal change had made things worse before it was walked back.

The cross-partisan pairing of Morano and Hudson on the portal and payment bills was a notable feature of the hearing, with Morano making a pointed but fair observation that the City is asking landlords to trust it as a payment partner while running multiple disconnected IT systems built piecemeal over a decade. The administration could not say when a unified landlord portal would be ready, only that enhancements were coming in the first quarter of 2027 and beyond. Several questions about basic program metrics, including how many landlords are corporate versus small individual owners, what percentage of packages are returned for correction, and whether any mechanism exists to flag or remove repeatedly non-compliant landlords, went unanswered. DSS confirmed there is currently no formal process for removing bad-actor landlords from the program, though a landlord escalation list focused on fraud and criminality is under development.

  • The top causes of inspection failure: water temperature failures (41 percent), electrical outlet deficiencies (33 percent), missing or unlabeled apartment numbers (19 percent), window guard and bracket violations (19 percent), refrigerator and freezer temperature failures (18 percent), missing or defective door self-closing mechanisms (17 percent), missing or outdated smoke or CO alarms (13 percent).
  • Average CityFHEPS application processing time from submission to approval is currently 24.8 days, down from 28 days approximately one to one-and-a-half months ago.
  • Processing time disaggregated: 21 days average for community-based applicants, 31 days for shelter residents.
  • Average time from shopping letter issuance to move-in: 244 days in 2024, 221 days in 2025, and 236 days year-to-date in 2026.
  • The CityFHEPS voucher shopping letter is valid for six months and is automatically renewed as long as income eligibility is maintained; document validity was recently extended from four months to six months.
  • The annual recertification retention rate for CityFHEPS voucher holders is approximately 90 percent.
  • Int 0101-2026 would require DSS to respond to applications within 15 days; the current average is 24.8 days.
  • Int 0641-2026 would require rental assistance payments no later than seven days before the first of the month; DSS currently issues City payments on the 26th of the prior month, with electronic fund transfer recipients receiving funds within 24 hours.
  • State shelter allowance payments are made in two tranches, at the beginning and middle of the month, meaning landlords may receive up to four separate payments per month in total.
