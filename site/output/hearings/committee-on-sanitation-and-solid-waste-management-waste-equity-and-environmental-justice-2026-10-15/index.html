Waste Equity and Environmental Justice

Committee on Sanitation and Solid Waste Management

Chair: Justin E. Sanchez
October 15, 2026 · 2hrs 21m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Committee on Sanitation and Solid Waste Management held an oversight hearing on the City's implementation of the Waste Equity Law (Local Law 152 of 2018), together with five bills covering containerization, battery disposal, composting, and litter basket policy. Chair Justin Sanchez (Bronx) opened with a pointed summary of the law's status: while citywide waste transfer volume has fallen 15% since passage, none of the four designated environmental justice community districts (Brooklyn CD1, Queens CD12, Bronx CD1, Bronx CD2) have achieved their mandated capacity reductions of 50% and 33% respectively. Brooklyn CD1 is at 35.8% reduction against a 50% target; Bronx CD1 at 14.8% against 33%; Queens CD12 at 23.9% against 33%. Bronx CD2 data was not specified. Despite this, 74% of citywide solid waste throughput remains concentrated in those four districts.

DSNY Commissioner Gregory Anderson testified that the department considers itself in full legal compliance, arguing that the law's exemption framework, which carved out capacity for recyclables processing, organics, construction and demolition recycling, and barge or rail export before applying percentage reductions, accounts for the gap between headline targets and actual numbers. Two Waste Management facilities in Bronx CD1 received no capacity reduction at all because they export nearly all waste by rail, which the law explicitly exempts. One facility, the Metropolitan Transfer Station, actually received a capacity increase in 2025 after adding organics processing infrastructure, a one-time credit permitted under the law. Chair Sanchez challenged this framing directly, noting that from the community's perspective the law's goals plainly have not been met, and pressed for the underlying exemption data to be provided to the Committee. Anderson also pointed to the commercial waste zones program (Local Law 199 of 2019), now live in six zones, as the most effective ongoing tool for reducing truck traffic in overburdened neighborhoods, projecting an 18 million vehicle-mile reduction annually once all 20 zones are operational by end of 2027.

On the five bills, the department's positions ranged from supportive-in-principle-but-not-quite to outright opposition. On Int 0030-2026 (containerization for City-owned buildings), DSNY argued all City buildings are already required to containerize and that mandating Empire Bins across every City facility would divert trucks and resources from the residential containerization rollout targeting large apartment buildings, which remains the priority through 2031. On Int 0987-2026 (allowing block associations to request shared containers), DSNY noted it already has a pilot application process in Brooklyn CD2 and welcomed further discussion. On Int 0352-2026 (battery disposal plan for micro-mobility devices), DSNY expressed broad agreement with the goal but flagged resource constraints, the limits of its special waste drop-off network (one site per borough), and ongoing negotiations with the Battery Network producer responsibility organisation whose plan has not yet been approved by DEP. On Int 0401-2026 (25 public organic waste receptacles per community district), DSNY flatly opposed it, citing a 42% drop in smart compost bin utilisation since mandatory curbside composting enforcement began, and arguing the money would be better spent on education and outreach. CM Restler pushed back, noting persistent demand at green market composting drop-offs and access problems for residents of large buildings with trash chutes. On Int 1041-2026 (restricting litter basket removals), Commissioner Anderson said the practice of removing baskets for illegal dumping had effectively ended before he took office in March 2026, and that he has not removed a single basket since becoming Commissioner, but resisted codification into law as removing the option of absolute last resort. CM Hankerson argued forcefully that the protection should be in statute regardless of who leads the agency, and that 3,300 approved litter basket locations across the city are currently missing a basket.

The hearing also surfaced several operational tensions. CM Hankerson pressed DSNY on why the four designated EJ districts were not prioritised first in the containerisation rollout; Commissioner Anderson gave a multi-factor answer involving borough diversity and geographic variety, and when Hankerson asked for a commitment that the next cohort after the current six districts would be the remaining three EJ districts, Anderson said only "we will certainly take a look at it," which Chair Sanchez immediately called a sidestep. Discussion of barge-based waste transfer and possible use of marine transfer stations for commercial waste, and even Rikers Island as an organic waste processing facility, rounded out the hearing's more speculative territory. The session closed with a reminder from Chair Sanchez that 311, not social media, is the correct channel for litter basket complaints.

  • The Action Environmental (now US Transportation) facility in Bronx CD1 had its permitted capacity reduced from 29.9 to 21.01 tons per day, with the gap from a straight 33% cut explained by a recycling exemption.
  • There are approximately 16 putrescible and 21 non-putrescible private waste transfer stations in New York City.
  • Commercial waste zones are projected to reduce truck traffic by 63%, equivalent to more than 18 million vehicle trips per year once all 20 zones are operational.
  • As of the end of Fiscal Year 2026, six commercial waste zones are fully operational; full implementation of all 20 zones is expected by end of 2027.
  • DSNY conducted 192,000 outreach activities and carriers conducted 2,581 individual employee trainings under the commercial waste zones programme.
  • DSNY has approximately 400 smart compost bins installed citywide; utilisation fell 42% in the year following mandatory curbside composting enforcement.
  • There are approximately 25,049 approved litter basket locations in New York City.
  • Approximately 3,300 approved litter basket locations are currently missing a basket.
  • DSNY now updates its litter basket Open Data inventory monthly rather than quarterly.
  • The newer Better Bin litter basket costs approximately $1,200 each; DSNY can purchase only a few hundred per year with its own funding.
  • DSNY currently offers special waste drop-off sites in one location per borough, open five days per week, accepting rechargeable batteries from mobility devices.
  • DSNY holds one SAFE disposal event per borough per year; a recent event during a nor'easter still drew over 800 attendees.
  • The illegal dumping fine for disposing of a substantial amount of waste from a vehicle is $4,000; improper disposal of household or commercial waste at a litter basket carries a $75 fine.
  • Full citywide residential containerisation is targeted for completion by 2031.
  • Community composting baseline funds were eliminated from the City budget in November 2023.
