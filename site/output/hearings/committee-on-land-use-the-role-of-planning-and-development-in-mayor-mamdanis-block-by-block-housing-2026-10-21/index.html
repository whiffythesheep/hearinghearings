The Role of Planning and Development in Mayor Mamdani's Block-by-Block Housing Plan

Committee on Land Use

Chair: Kevin C. Riley
October 21, 2026 · 3hrs 37m · Watch on councilnyc.viebit.com ↗ · View on council.nyc.gov ↗

Meeting overview

The Land Use Committee held a combined session on October 21, 2026, first approving six land use items by voice vote before pivoting to an oversight hearing on Mayor Mamdani's Block-by-Block Housing Plan. The land use approvals were routine: LU 0129 and 0130 covered UDAAP and Article XI tax exemption approvals for a four-building affordable homeownership preservation cluster at 48-50 Convent Avenue in West Harlem (approximately 77 units); LU 0142 and 0143 approved a rezoning from R5 to C4-5D with a Mandatory Inclusionary Housing text amendment at 1455 Coney Island Avenue in Brooklyn for a senior long-term care facility; and LU 0144 and 0145 approved a rezoning from R4 to R6A with MIH at 135-27 Sapphire Street in Queens for 268 apartments, up to 80 of which will be permanently affordable. All six passed 10-0.

The bulk of the hearing was a substantive interrogation of the Block-by-Block Housing Plan, which targets 400,000 affordable housing units over a decade (200,000 new, 200,000 preserved) as part of a broader goal of 700,000 total new units citywide. Chair Riley set the tone early: the Council supports the goals but is frustrated by the absence of detail on how the administration intends to achieve them, particularly on infrastructure investment, community planning and the transfer of final ULURP approval authority from elected Council members to the appointed City Planning Commission in the 12 designated Affordable Housing Fast Track districts. City Planning Commission Chair Cindy Sherman and HPD Deputy Commissioner Michael Sandler defended the plan, emphasising streamlined pre-certification timelines (two years down to six months for SEQRA-exempt applications), the new sprint team to process exempt applications, two launched neighbourhood plans (White Plains Road in the Bronx and South of Prospect Park in Brooklyn), and the Fair Housing Growth Strategy as a legally mandated framework for distributing housing targets across community districts. They acknowledged repeatedly that the fast track list had only been released earlier that month and that specific plans for the 12 districts had not yet been developed.

The Council's concerns clustered around several persistent themes. First, infrastructure: member after member from flood-prone, transit-poor or built-out districts (southeast Queens, Bay Ridge, Staten Island, the Upper West Side, Middle Village) described crumbling sewers, outdated power lines, school seat deficits and regular flooding, and argued that fast-tracking housing approvals into these areas without capital investment is irresponsible. Second, the loss of Council leverage: CM Carr articulated most precisely what several members felt, namely that the Council has historically used its final vote in ULURP to extract infrastructure commitments and deeper affordability, and that the City Planning Commission has neither the track record nor the institutional tools to replicate that. Sherman's response, that the Commission can recommend modifications and that pre-certification engagement will substitute for final-vote leverage, did not visibly reassure sceptical members. Third, displacement: multiple members and public witnesses flagged that upzoning in already-affordable, largely built-out neighbourhoods risks demolishing rent-stabilised units without replacing them, particularly where MIH rents at 80% AMI exceed prevailing market rents, as CM Zhuang documented for her district and CM Santosuosso for Bay Ridge. CM Brewer, representing the Upper West Side, was characteristically blunt, contending the methodology is absurd applied to a neighbourhood with almost no vacant land and a net loss of 15,000 rent-stabilised units over 15 years, and threatening litigation.

Specific sub-issues of note included: CM Marte's concern that the 100 Gold Street project in Lower Manhattan, which could produce 4,000 units, is being used as a vehicle to map an R30 zoning district across the Special Lower Manhattan District without adequate community process, effectively bypassing comprehensive planning for an area that could see multiple supertall developments; CM Abreu's alarm at warehoused, rezoned sites in East Harlem that remain unbuilt a decade later while new tools are being added without resolving the original problem; CM Thomas-Henry's pitch for Willets Point as a housing site; the Spotlight on the Bronx initiative, which HPD described as a quality-of-life intervention focused on existing affordable housing conditions rather than new construction, with community board conversations promised before year end; and the library co-location pipeline, where Riley identified three additional sites (Parkchester, Marcy and Sunnyside) that the administration has not yet formally accepted into its programme alongside its existing three committed sites.

Public testimony was broadly aligned with the Council's scepticism. The Association for Neighborhood and Housing Development, the Municipal Art Society, the Brooklyn Borough President's office, the Historic Districts Council, the New York Landmarks Conservancy and the Thriving Communities Coalition all supported housing growth but called for comprehensive neighbourhood planning, deeper affordability targets, displacement protections before rezoning and a clearer implementation framework connecting the Block-by-Block plan, the Fair Housing Growth Strategy and the fast track. Open New York and the New York Housing Conference were more enthusiastic about supply-side measures and neighbourhood rezonings. Three panels of industry witnesses from the plumbing and construction trades testified against a proposal on page 99 of Block-by-Block to expand plastic piping in buildings taller than five stories, citing fire safety and public health concerns; a building inspectors union flagged that inspector staffing across DOB, HPD and DOB would need to be substantially increased for any housing plan to be safely implemented; and Local 78 called for professional certification in asbestos permitting to reduce delays that currently add months to the start of construction projects.

▸ Numbers
  • Block-by-Block Housing Plan targets 200,000 new affordable units and 200,000 preservation units, totalling 400,000 affordable units over the next decade.
  • The broader housing production goal is 700,000 total new units over ten years, a rate described as greater than anything achieved in New York City in the past hundred years.
  • The Fair Housing Growth Strategy citywide affordable housing target is 25% of new production, with 7.3% at deeply affordable levels.
  • Of HPD-financed affordable housing, 50% is targeted at extremely low and very low incomes.
  • The 12 Affordable Housing Fast Track districts represent approximately 20% of the City's community districts but produced just 1% of the City's affordable housing over the last five years.
  • SEQRA exemption applies to projects of 250 units or fewer in low-to-mid density districts and 500 units or fewer in higher density districts.
  • A ten-year analysis found fewer than 1% of projects subject to SEQRA review produced an environmental impact requiring mitigation.
  • The SEQRA review process was estimated to add approximately $80,000 in additional costs per unit.
  • DCP is targeting a reduction in pre-certification time from approximately two years to six months for SEQRA-exempt applications.
  • One recent project in Flushing reached public review approximately one year earlier as a result of the SEQRA exemption.
  • DCP currently has approximately 13 applications already identified as SEQRA-exempt in its pipeline.
  • DCP has a current overall vacancy rate of approximately 24%, largely driven by 46 new unfilled positions.
  • DCP has posted 28 of the 46 new positions and has nine candidates in the pipeline for other vacancies.
  • The East New York Neighbourhood Plan produced over 6,000 units of housing in ten years, more than 50% affordable, alongside hundreds of millions of dollars of City investment.
  • City-led neighbourhood rezonings from 2010 to 2020 produced nearly three times more new housing units than private development applications.
  • Over the last decade, neighbourhood rezonings enabled nearly 80,000 additional housing units.
  • The prior mayoral term completed five neighbourhood rezonings; the current administration has launched two.
  • Typical neighbourhood rezonings in the prior administration ranged from 1,000 to 4,000 new units; larger plans included Bronx Metro-North at approximately 7,000 units and Atlantic Avenue at approximately 5,000 units.
  • The proposed 100 Gold Street project in Lower Manhattan is projected at approximately 4,000 units.
  • HPD's affordable homeownership down payment assistance programme is being doubled from approximately 100 to 300 recipients per year and eligibility expanded from 80% to 120% AMI.
  • HPD's Open Door programme for affordable co-ops is doubling production targets from 150 to 300 units per year.
  • The Bronx has less than 10% homeownership rate.
  • New York City's housing vacancy rate stands at 1.4% overall and 0.4% for deeply affordable units.
  • 86% of New Yorkers earning under $50,000 per year are rent-burdened; 68% are severely rent-burdened.
  • Community District 8 (Upper East Side) lost 14,793 rent-stabilised apartments between 2008 and 2025.
  • Community District 10 (Bay Ridge) has 14,909 rent-stabilised units and a school seat deficit of approximately 4,000 despite five new schools already built.
  • Since 2023, over 19,000 units have been permitted through conversion projects citywide, none of which are counted in the administration's housing production metrics.
  • HPD design standards require a minimum of 30% two-to-three-bedroom units and a maximum of 25% studios; current HPD-financed projects average approximately 41% two-or-more-bedroom units.
  • The City invested $20 million in underground infrastructure in Hollis, Queens, which residents report did not alleviate flooding.
  • A virtual testifier cited an exemption affecting nearly 1,100 units at an estimated cost to the City of over $359 million with no public disclosure to tenants.
