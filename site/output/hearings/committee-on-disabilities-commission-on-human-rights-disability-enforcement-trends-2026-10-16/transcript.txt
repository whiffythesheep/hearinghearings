Millions of New Yorkers live with disabilities, and disability discrimination is now the most common basis of claims filed with the Commission on Human Rights. New York City has some of the strongest civil rights protections in the country. Our human rights law provides broader protections for people with disabilities than state or federal law, including the right to reasonable accommodations and a good faith cooperative dialog with employers, landlords and businesses regarding their accessibility needs. But strong protections on paper mean very little if New Yorkers cannot access them in practice. Without meaningful enforcement, our promises are unfulfilled.

The Commission on Human Rights issued a ruling in a case involving a tenant who spent more than four years requesting a ramp or lift so he could safely leave his apartment. Accessibility delayed is accessibility denied. In some cases those delays have irreversible consequences.

This is precisely why today's hearing matters. A December 2025 audit by the New York State Comptroller found that housing discrimination cases took more than two years on average to reach a determination. The Commission's own data also show an aging caseload, yet the agency does not publicly report how long disability discrimination cases take to resolve or what outcomes complainants ultimately receive.

Today we intend to examine several critical questions. One: how will the Commission's 2026 reorganization improve intake, investigations and resolution timelines for disability discrimination complaints? Two: what concrete steps has the agency taken to implement the State Comptroller's recommendations and address its growing backlog? Three: how are the Commission on Human Rights and the Mayor's Office for People with Disabilities coordinating to ensure complaints are addressed effectively and that New Yorkers are not left navigating fragmented systems? Four: how is the Commission on Human Rights translating disability discrimination guidance into meaningful enforcement, education and compliance? And finally: what additional resources, data transparency or policy reforms are necessary to ensure that New Yorkers receive timely relief?

Today I will provide a brief overview of the City Human Rights Law's disability protections as well as enforcement and outreach. More details on the specifics are in my written testimony. The Commission's enforcement includes investigating complaints as well as seeking to resolve matters before a formal complaint is filed where appropriate, and securing remedies for violations of the law. The Commission also provides trainings and develops know-your-rights and know-your-obligations materials so that New Yorkers and covered entities understand their rights and responsibilities under the City Human Rights Law.

These functions complement one another. Community engagement promotes awareness of the law and access to the Commission, while our enforcement removes barriers to accessibility and protects the rights of persons with disabilities. Our engagement and enforcement support the end goals of obtaining individual remedies and preventing future discrimination. The Human Rights Law aims to achieve independent and equal access to all aspects of City life for New Yorkers, and broadly protects people with disabilities from discrimination at work, in their living spaces and in public places. To this end, the law prohibits employers, housing providers and places of public accommodation from discriminating against individuals because of an actual or perceived disability. Service providers and housing providers must ensure full and equal enjoyment of their facilities and services regardless of disability, and employers are prohibited from treating employees less well...

(00:18:17)

On the basis of disability, the City Human Rights Law also requires covered entities to provide reasonable accommodations for people with disabilities unless doing so would impose an undue hardship. These include changes to policies or practices, workplace adjustments, or the installation of ramps or lifts to make a building accessible. As noted, part of the process of providing a reasonable accommodation is the obligation to have a cooperative dialog with an individual to help identify their needs and how to meet them.

The Commission on Human Rights has various enforcement tools so the agency can respond to the circumstances of individual matters and, where appropriate, address broader patterns of potential discrimination. In fiscal year 2026, disability claims were the most common type of claim across the areas of employment, housing and public accommodations, which is similar to recent years' trends. Complaints lead to an investigation by the agency's Office of Civil Rights Enforcement. In order to remove barriers faced by individuals with disabilities and foster inclusive and accessible spaces, the Commission employs, in addition to complaint investigations and litigation, other tools. Where physical accessibility is at issue, the Commission has a project, Equal Access, that works with housing providers and businesses to identify how to make spaces accessible, delivering faster solutions to persons with disabilities including grab bars, ramps and lifts.

The Commission on Human Rights also proactively identifies potential discrimination through testing to assess compliance with the Human Rights Law in a range of areas, which include identifying potential barriers to accessibility. Together, pre-complaint interventions, testing and resolution of individual complaints are critical for securing meaningful relief and promoting compliance with the City Human Rights Law. When the Commission identifies violations of the law, covered entities need to take action such as modifications or policy changes, and can also be ordered to pay damages to individuals and to pay civil penalties. The agency also seeks remedies to prevent future discrimination and promote an accessible city. In employment and public accommodations, respondents can be required to create reasonable accommodation policies and post notices so employees and patrons know their rights.

I want to highlight that in fiscal year 2026, case resolutions also played a role in ensuring New Yorkers with disabilities were able to access their housing. We heard about one example already. In another matter, the Commission was able to assist an individual whose landlord had refused to enter into a cooperative dialog or install a ramp after their mobility became limited. As a result, the tenant had to stay longer than needed in a rehab facility because he could not enter his building. The Commission ultimately secured an agreement to compel the housing provider to build a ramp, and the complainant was awarded $102,000 in emotional distress damages as well as reimbursed back rent. In another matter, housing providers that failed to accommodate an individual ultimately agreed to provide 14 months of rental credit and to transfer the individual to an accessible apartment.

These case outcomes demonstrate that disability enforcement resolutions are tailored for an array of circumstances. The Commission on Human Rights' enforcement is also coupled with educating New Yorkers about the City Human Rights Law in order to prevent discrimination. The agency provides disability rights education through its Human Rights Law 101 trainings and through more focused programming. The Commission also works with community-based organizations, disability advocates, elected officials, businesses, housing providers and other partners to increase awareness of the law. Community engagement helps New Yorkers recognize potential discrimination and allows the Commission on Human Rights to reach communities through trusted partners, which includes bringing information about rights and resources directly into neighborhoods across the City.

In fiscal year 2026, the Commission continued to leverage community partnerships to advance the agency's mandate through events large and small.
