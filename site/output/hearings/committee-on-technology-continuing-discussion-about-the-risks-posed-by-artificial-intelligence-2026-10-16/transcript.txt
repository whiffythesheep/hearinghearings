Thank you, Speaker Menin. Good morning, everyone. I am Council Member Carmen De La Rosa and I am the Chair of the Committee on Technology. Welcome to our hearing on continuing discussions about the risks posed by artificial intelligence.

Last week in this very same room, in a meeting of the Committee of the Whole, we heard testimony from nearly 100 witnesses, including representatives from OpenAI, Meta, Anthropic and Google, their former employees, technical experts, academics, elected officials and members of the public. Despite their different points of view, their message was more or less consistent: the pace and progress of frontier models does pose a risk. Some called for frontier developers to slow down. Others focused on alignment and control mechanisms. Warnings of superintelligence, mass surveillance, energetic policing as well as political bias and discriminatory tendencies — several witnesses raised concerns about the loss of human control and others warned about the potential loss of jobs.

However, much of the testimony returned to a common set of recommendations: transparency from developers, credible safety measures, third-party evaluators and protection for personal data. Today we plan to continue this conversation with another major company, SpaceX AI. We want to reap the benefits of AI so long as these benefits come with responsible development and do not cost us our safety, our dignity and our civil rights. AI can be a powerful tool for efficiency and innovation in science, medicine and productivity. At the same time it introduces important risks, including bias, discrimination, job displacement, censorship, digital forgeries and fakes — concerns that become even more serious as AI agents become more capable.

We should make sure tools work as intended, that we understand how they work, how they were trained, and where there is an opportunity to contest decisions made by machines. A framework where the very same people who build the tools and have competing motives are also expected to regulate themselves is wholly insufficient. Intervention is necessary from federal, state and municipal governments to protect our rights.

Once again, I look forward to hearing more from industry experts about what solutions are possible to ensure New Yorkers can fully benefit from technological advancement without compromising civil rights, safety and privacy.
