Summary

Meeting Overview

The Committee on Higher Education, chaired by CM Rita Joseph, held a joint hearing with the Committee on Education, chaired by CM Eric Dinowitz, on the New York State Seal of Civic Readiness and its implementation across DOE high schools and CUNY. The hearing also addressed Int 0259-2026 (CM Krishnan), which would require the DOE to distribute materials on the importance of dental care and fluoride, framed explicitly as a response to federal-level disinformation about public health. The primary legislative items were Res 0418-2026, calling on CUNY to award college credits for the Seal, and Proposed Res. 620-A, calling on the DOE to ensure the Seal is awarded in all NYC public high schools. Res 0451-2026, sponsored by CM Zhuang, called on the State Legislature to pass the Next-Gen Civics Act mandating hands-on civic education in grades nine through twelve.

The Seal of Civic Readiness, operational statewide since 2022, requires students to accumulate six points from a menu of civic knowledge and participation benchmarks, including advanced social studies coursework, Regents exams, service learning, extracurriculars and a capstone project. DOE's Cordelia Viv and colleagues testified that the program has grown dramatically, from 682 students at 31 schools in its pilot year to 12,019 students across 230 schools in 2025-26, with 271 schools now participating in 2026-27. They described the Civics for All initiative, launched in 2018 under Mayor de Blasio, as the broader infrastructure underpinning the Seal, covering 35 of 45 districts in a partner-district model. The testimony was broadly positive about the program's expansion, though the committee pressed hard on equity gaps: the Seal is currently offered in only about 56% of diploma-granting schools, and which civic participation opportunities are available varies by school, with little current data on whether this correlates with poverty level, race or disability status.

CUNY's Alyssa Vine, University Director of Credit for Prior Learning, testified that no CUNY college has yet evaluated the Seal of Civic Readiness for credit, and that CUNY does not currently receive data from the DOE on which incoming students have earned it. She outlined a plausible path forward: assembling faculty from around eight colleges to evaluate the Seal, with particular focus on the high school capstone project as the most promising candidate for credit equivalency. She drew a useful comparison to the Seal of Biliteracy, for which 12 CUNY colleges now have credit pathways in place, but noted that the Civic Readiness Seal is harder to assess because its pathways are intentionally varied rather than tied to a single measurable standard. The committee and witnesses agreed the obvious next step is a formal CUNY-DOE meeting to review student work products, and Dinowitz extracted an on-the-record commitment to schedule it.

Several recurring frustrations emerged from the hearing. The Seal does not appear as a searchable filter on MySchools, the public school enrollment portal parents use to choose schools, a gap the DOE committed to fixing. STARS, the student data system, can only record one seal per student, meaning students who earn both the Seal of Biliteracy and the Seal of Civic Readiness may be undercounted in official reporting. The DOE acknowledged it is building a Seal tracker in STARS that will eventually migrate to OASIS, but the timeline and whether OASIS will handle multiple seals remains unclear. More broadly, both chairs and CM Brewer expressed exasperation at the persistent public perception that NYC public schools do not teach civics at all, contrasting it with the extensive and largely unknown programming documented in the hearing. The messaging and outreach problem was discussed at length but without producing concrete commitments beyond the DOE agreeing to share its newsletter and continue panelist appearances.

Numbers

- 12,019 students graduated with the Seal of Civic Readiness in the 2025-26 school year, across 230 high schools.
- 271 schools are participating in the Seal program in 2026-27, the largest number to date.
- 682 students at 31 schools earned the Seal in the 2021-22 pilot year, representing a 1,600% increase over five years.
- 8,000 students from 166 DOE high schools earned the Seal in 2024-25, a 72% increase from the prior year.
- 643 graduating seniors from 31 high schools earned the Seal at the end of the 2022 pilot year.
- The Seal is currently offered in approximately 56.2% of diploma-granting NYC public high schools.
- 35 of NYC Public Schools' 45 districts participate in the Civics for All partner-district program.
- 12 CUNY colleges have credit pathways in place for the Seal of Biliteracy, a comparable but distinct credential.
- More than 60% of NYC high school graduates who go on to college enroll at CUNY.
- To earn the Seal, students must accumulate 6 points from 11 possible criteria, with at least 2 from civic knowledge and 2 from civic participation benchmarks.
- Service learning for the Seal requires at least 25 hours; extracurricular or work-based learning requires at least 40 hours.

- The overall Civics for All budget is $3,001,659.

Action Points

- CUNY and NYC Public Schools to schedule a formal meeting to review student Seal of Civic Readiness work products, with CUNY faculty evaluating capstone projects and other outputs for potential college credit pathways.
- CUNY to convene a faculty working group of approximately eight colleges to formally evaluate the Seal of Civic Readiness for Credit for Prior Learning purposes, beginning with the high school capstone project.
- CUNY and NYC Public Schools to establish a data-sharing agreement so CUNY receives information on incoming students who have earned the Seal of Civic Readiness.
- CUNY to investigate whether the Seal of Civic Readiness notation under state program service code 8013 can be captured in its central admissions process, and to report back to the committee.
- CUNY to create a public-facing website listing colleges that award credit for the Seal of Civic Readiness, mirroring the existing Seal of Biliteracy credit page, once credit pathways are established.
- NYC Public Schools to work with the MySchools enrollment portal team to add the Seal of Civic Readiness as a searchable and visible filter for parents and students choosing high schools.
- NYC Public Schools to provide the committee with demographic data on Seal earners and participating schools broken down by race, ethnicity, income, disability status, borough and school type including District 75 and transfer schools.
- NYC Public Schools to follow up with the Office of Policy Evaluation on the timeline for transitioning from STARS to OASIS and whether OASIS will support multiple seal codes per student.
- NYC Public Schools to confirm how many seventh and eighth graders completed a middle school capstone project in 2025-26 and report back to the committee.
- NYC Public Schools to follow up on how many schools are incorporating the Black Studies curriculum within their social studies and civics instruction, given the council's investment in that curriculum.
- NYC Public Schools to send CM Brewer its Civics for All newsletter and explore how external youth civic organizations including those listed on the Youth Civics Hub can formally accredit their programs for Seal purposes.
- NYC Public Schools to share voter registration comics and other electoral civic education materials with CM Farias and to facilitate her participation in Civics Week school activities in her district.
- NYC Public Schools to provide CM Dinowitz with the link to the public accreditation portal so journalism programs can be submitted for review as accredited civics projects.
- Superintendent Inzerillo (District 26) to include a marker at the November 9 District 26 high school fair indicating which high schools offer the Seal of Civic Readiness and the Seal of Biliteracy.
- NYC Public Schools to share data with the committee on whether civic participation opportunities available under the Seal differ by school poverty level, once tracking capacity allows.
- NYC Public Schools to report back to the committee on the status of the Seal of Civic Readiness tracker being built in STARS Classroom and its planned migration to OASIS.

The finalized statewide definition of civic readiness includes four domains of readiness intended to be developed across grades K through 12: civic knowledge, civic skills in action, civic mindsets and civic experiences. Once established, the Seal of Civic Readiness was approved by the Board of Regents in 2021 and piloted across the state, including at New York City Public Schools. The launch of the NYC Seal of Civic Readiness complemented our citywide civics education initiative by offering a formal recognition that a student has demonstrated proficiency in the domains of civic readiness. In order to earn the Seal of Civic Readiness, a student must earn six points from a list of criteria that demonstrate a student's proficiency in civic knowledge and civic participation. Two of the student's points must be from the civic knowledge criteria, two must be from the civic participation criteria and the remaining two can be from either one. There are 11 possible criteria, each earning a student between 0.5 and 4 points.

The intent behind this structure is to maximize the way a student's strengths and school experience can earn them points towards the Seal. It allows schools to develop their own routes to the Seal using those criteria, building on their school's distinctive context and existing programming while also supporting students' needs aligned to their school's culture. For example, a school with a law program in which students take civics-related electives has the option to accredit these electives and ensure students can earn points by taking them.
