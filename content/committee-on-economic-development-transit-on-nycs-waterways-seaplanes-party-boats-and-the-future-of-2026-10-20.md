Summary

Meeting Overview

The joint hearing of the Committees on Economic Development and Transportation and Infrastructure, chaired by Virginia Maloney and Majority Leader Shaun Abreu, covered four broad topics: the state and future of New York City Ferry, proposed new ferry routes, seaplane operations at the East 23rd Street Skyport, and party boat safety. The administration was represented primarily by James Wong, Executive Vice President and Executive Director of Ferry at NYC EDC, and Anton Frederickson from DOT, who fielded a lengthy round of questions from council members. Four bills proposing new ferry service or feasibility studies were on the agenda: Int 0704-2026 (Canarsie Pier), Int 0846-2026 (Willets Point), Int 1022-2026 (West 125th Street) and Int 1032-2026 (City Island). The administration declined to support any of them, arguing that the ongoing ferry vision study, expected to conclude by summer 2027, is the right vehicle for evaluating expansion proposals comprehensively.

On the ferry system itself, EDC presented an operationally optimistic picture. Ridership has grown, the per-rider subsidy has fallen from nearly $13 in fiscal year 2020 to $8.79 today, and the 2025 route optimization produced a 6% overall ridership increase with a 17% jump on the East River route and 9% of all trips now representing connections that did not previously exist. The base fare rose from $4 to $5 on the day before the hearing. The operating contract with Hornblower expires in 2028 with two one-year extension options already priced in; the administration said it was too early to decide whether to extend or re-procure. Council members across the hearing pushed on the contract's transparency, advertising and sponsorship revenues, and the KPI framework, and pressed EDC to share contract documents proactively rather than requiring FOIL requests. Chair Maloney flagged explicitly that a procurement decision of this scale should not be made without the Council and the public at the table.

On the four expansion bills, the administration was consistent in its scepticism. All four proposed sites sit well outside the existing service area, each would cost roughly $5 to $6 million per year to operate at minimum frequency, and together they would require eight additional vessels and $25 to $30 million in annual operating costs, representing a more than 30% increase in the ferry budget. Capital costs could reach hundreds of millions. Canarsie faces National Park Service jurisdiction and an estimated $30 million pier rehabilitation; Willets Point has shallow water and mud flat problems; West 125th Street needs new vessels since it is over four miles outside the current service area; and City Island has infrastructure and environmental challenges. Council members were not satisfied with these answers. CM Banks made an impassioned case for Canarsie as a transit desert facing additional housing density with no corresponding infrastructure investment. CM Gutiérrez argued that Willets Point's transformation into a major housing and stadium destination has fundamentally changed the calculus. CM Abreu pressed for West 125th Street and offered capital funding partnership. CM Louis and CM Riley made detailed cases for City Island, emphasising vacancy rates, economic development potential and the Hart Island access problem. CM Brooks-Powers pushed for the eastern Rockaways to be included in the vision study. Several council members noted that the vision study is already running late and extracted a commitment from the administration to deliver by summer 2027.

The seaplane and party boat sections were shorter but substantive. Chair Maloney pressed hard on the July 2025 hard landing incident involving Arcadian Seaplanes, the enforcement of the 1,000-foot offshore takeoff rule, the ban on flying north of the 59th Street Bridge, and noise abatement. Answers were often thin: DOT acknowledged it would have to follow up on how often operators had violated rules, what penalties had been imposed, and the status of Community Board 6 resolutions dating back to 1996. The seaplane base generates approximately $300,000 per year for the City. On party boats, the administration described its oversight framework through the DOCS program managed by Billy Babe Marina Services, but acknowledged that investigations into specific incidents involving operators who caused injuries remained with federal regulators. Council members found the division of responsibility between DOT, EDC, DOCS and federal agencies difficult to parse and questioned whether the City is using the full range of tools it already has, pointing to its precedent of restricting helicopter tourist flights at the Downtown Manhattan Heliport a decade ago.
