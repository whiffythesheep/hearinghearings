Summary

Meeting Overview

The Joint Committee on Fire and Emergency Management and Hospitals held an oversight hearing on ambulance response times in New York City, alongside consideration of Int 0990-2026, sponsored by Chair Ariola, which would require the City to issue Notify NYC emergency notifications whenever FDNY has more than 25 pending ambulance dispatches. The hearing was framed around a genuine crisis: average citywide ambulance response times have reached 13 minutes and 9 seconds, against a background of rising call volumes, falling ambulance availability, and an EMS workforce that is badly understaffed, badly paid, and bleeding personnel at an alarming rate.

FDNY Chief of Department John Esposito led the department's testimony, accompanied by Chief of EMS Paul Mariano and Chief of EMS Dispatch Denise Werner. Their account was candid to a degree unusual for agency testimony: they acknowledged the system is in crisis, that they are responding to more calls with fewer ambulances than five years ago, and that the root cause is a staffing collapse driven primarily by uncompetitive pay. Average ambulance in-service hours per day have fallen from 8,891 in fiscal year 2022 to 8,109 in fiscal year 2026, while life-threatening call volume remains near historic highs. Over 90 ambulances per day are out of service, overwhelmingly due to lack of staff to crew them. The department is currently 410 to 464 personnel below its budgeted headcount of 4,500. A major compounding factor is a promotion cycle pulling EMTs and paramedics into the firefighter pipeline: 358 EMTs and 63 paramedics departed in 2026 alone. Hospital closures - 19 since 2003 - have lengthened transport distances and reduced the number of hospital-run units in the 911 system. The department outlined a range of mitigations including paramedic response units, a centralized resource coordination centre, borough-based command structures, mutual aid agreements, and early-stage AI deployment optimisation, as well as work on a new bespoke scheduling system and a CAD system upgrade with the Office of Technology and Innovation. On Int 0990-2026, FDNY expressed scepticism that blanket Notify NYC alerts would provide useful information without causing public confusion, but said it was open to further discussion on how to provide more real-time information to callers.

The hearing opened with testimony from Jennifer Daly, whose 14-year-old son showed signs of a stroke at St. Francis Prep in Fresh Meadows. The school nurse called 911 three times and no ambulance arrived; her family drove him to New York-Presbyterian Queens and he was subsequently transferred to Morgan Stanley Children's Hospital. FDNY's account revealed that during that period the system was fielding over 100 calls above normal volume, the call went in as an unknown condition because no triage dispatcher was available to answer it, and while an ambulance was dispatched it arrived after the family had already left. Speaker Julie Menin described a separate incident in her district where a 16-year-old injured during a basketball game prompted five 911 calls with no response, despite a firehouse next door; FDNY called back nearly three hours later to ask if an ambulance was still needed. The firehouse proximity was explained by the fact that fire companies are only dispatched to segment one through three life-threatening calls, not to injuries classified as segment four or five. Both incidents illustrated the core problem: when ambulances are scarce, triaging decisions about who gets one become brutal, and lower-acuity calls that would still benefit from timely care get dropped or heavily delayed. Multiple council members pressed for public-facing information so that callers in non-life-threatening but still serious situations could make their own decisions, such as arranging private transport, rather than simply waiting without knowing help may not be coming.

NYC Health and Hospitals was represented by an emergency physician who fielded questions from Chair Narcisse, a former triage nurse at Elmhurst Hospital. The H+H testimony was considerably thinner than FDNY's: the representative was unable to provide data on which hospitals have the longest wait times, the volume of patients leaving against medical advice, the results of several named programmes, or the impact of the Mount Sinai Beth Israel closure. What emerged was that median time from arrival to first provider across H+H facilities is 29 minutes, improved from 33 minutes previously, but that time from decision-to-admit to inpatient bed is approximately 270 minutes citywide. Hospital overcrowding, driven partly by primary care gaps, Medicaid cuts, and the closure of facilities, is keeping ambulances tied up at hospitals longer than necessary. Programmes discussed included Virtual ExpressCare, which diverts some 911 calls to telehealth and avoids ambulance dispatch, and the LEADS programme, which addresses substance use in the emergency department. The committee pressed on the B-HEARTS mental health response programme, the Bridge to Home supportive housing initiative, and the SHOW outreach programme, with the H+H representative repeatedly unable to provide detail and promising written follow-up.

The dominant theme running through the entire hearing was pay. Starting wages for EMTs are $18 an hour. Average tenure is under three years. Some workers are living in shelters or sleeping in cars. The City Council - including the Speaker, the committee chairs, and virtually every member who questioned witnesses - was unified in arguing that the contract currently being negotiated between the EMT union and the Office of Labor Relations is the single most important lever available, and that without a meaningful pay increase the recruitment and retention crisis will not be resolved. The Fire Commissioner and department chiefs agreed publicly and repeatedly. Chair Ariola closed by arguing that EMS workers should additionally be granted uniformed first responder status and civil service exam pathways for promotion, and expressed confidence that current political conditions represent the best opportunity in memory to deliver a genuinely competitive contract.

Numbers

- Average citywide ambulance response time is currently 13 minutes and 9 seconds.
- Average ambulance in-service hours per day fell from 8,891 in fiscal year 2022 to 8,572 in fiscal year 2025 and 8,109 in fiscal year 2026.
- Life-threatening medical calls totalled 564,412 in fiscal year 2022; over 480,000 segment one through three calls have already been recorded in the current fiscal year to date.
- Over 90 ambulances per day are currently out of service, overwhelmingly due to staffing shortages.
- FDNY EMS is 410 to 464 personnel below its budgeted headcount of 4,500.
- 358 EMTs and 63 paramedics left EMS for the firefighter promotion cycle in 2026; 288 EMTs and 48 paramedics did so in 2025.
- Starting pay for FDNY EMTs is $18 an hour.
- Average length of service for an FDNY EMT is just under three years.
- EMS has approximately 4,500 budgeted personnel compared to FDNY fire suppression's approximately 13,000, responding to roughly double the call volume.
- 19 hospitals have closed in New York City since 2003, including one in 2025.
- The 911 ambulance system is approximately two thirds municipal FDNY units and one third hospital-run units.
- FDNY targets 640 ambulance tours per day, running approximately 328 in the day period and 312 in the evening period.
- There are currently 163 EMT trainees at the FDNY academy at Fort Totten, down from a starting class of 200, plus approximately 150 in paramedic training.
- FDNY operates 14 paramedic response units across the city.
- Approximately 38% of daily 911 calls are classified as high acuity or life-threatening; the remaining 62% are not.
- Median time from emergency department arrival to first provider at NYC Health and Hospitals facilities is 29 minutes, improved from 33 minutes previously.
- Average time from decision-to-admit to inpatient bed transfer at H+H is approximately 270 minutes.
- Between 2022 and 2025, patients at H+H hospitals waited an average of 12.8 hours to be transferred from the emergency department to an inpatient bed.
- H+H treated over one million emergency department patients across its 11 facilities in 2025, with increases concentrated in higher-acuity cases.
- H+H psychiatric emergency services treated 33,400 unique patients across 56,600 visits in 2025, up slightly from 33,000 unique patients and 55,000 visits previously reported.
- The LEADS substance use programme recorded 30,420 encounters in 2025, up from 22,500 in 2023, with over 6,500 referrals to outpatient clinics and over 3,600 naloxone kits distributed.
- Fire companies conduct approximately 1,000 certified first responder medical emergency runs per 24-hour period citywide.
- The Hospital Liaison Unit pilot operated at 13 hospitals and reduced turnaround time by approximately two minutes citywide before being discontinued.
- Lincoln Hospital emergency department receives approximately 37 patients per hour at peak.
- Patients leaving H+H emergency departments without being seen represent approximately 2% of visits.
