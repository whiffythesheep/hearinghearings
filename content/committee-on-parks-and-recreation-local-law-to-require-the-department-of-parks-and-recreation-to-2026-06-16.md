Summary

Meeting Overview

The Parks and Recreation Committee held a hearing on six bills covering street tree transparency, tree removal permitting, park signage for dog waste, a dog waste composting pilot, and an administrative fix for honorary street naming biographical records. The session was substantive but not decisive — no votes were taken and all bills remain works in progress, with Parks opposing or expressing reservations about most of them while offering to continue discussions with sponsors.

Three of the six bills concerned street trees. Int 0089-2026 (CM Brewer) would require Parks to map all tree pits, occupied or vacant, on the City's online tree map and note why empty ones are unused. Parks opposed it, arguing the data does not currently exist in a usable form, that collecting it would be a massive logistical undertaking diverting staff from actual planting, and that their new Neighborhood Tree Planting Program already addresses the underlying problem by systematically surveying and replanting entire blocks. CM Morano and CM Brewer pushed back, arguing that if Parks already has partial data then withholding it from residents is indefensible, and that volunteers could help collect what is missing. Int 0576-2026 would require certified arborist credentials for tree removal permit applicants and annual reporting on removals. Parks worried — somewhat implausibly — that adding this requirement might push some people to skip the permit process entirely, and distinguished between arborist credentials (suited to care decisions) and the physical act of removal. They were more open to the reporting aspect. Int 0666-2026 would require Parks to publish its internal maintenance prioritization ratings for individual trees on its public tree map. Parks expressed support in principle, objecting only to a 30-day compliance window they called unrealistic for website development work.

The two dog waste bills generated the liveliest exchange. Int 0880-2026 (CM Narcisse) would require signs at all park entrances and relevant facilities stating the rules and penalties for leaving dog waste, and mandate signage responses to 311 complaints. Parks said it already posts consolidated rules at entrances and specific signage at dog runs, and was broadly supportive in principle but flagged that the 311-triggered signage requirement, which technically falls partly on the Department of Transportation, would be unworkable at scale — potentially mandating thousands of signs for complaints that may already be resolved by the time installation happens. The numbers from the hearing were telling: Parks issued roughly 20 to 22 dog waste summonses over the past several years citywide, against approximately 1,400 to 1,500 dog waste complaints on park properties annually and 25,000 to 26,000 dog waste complaints citywide. CM Narcisse's dry conclusion — that the near-zero enforcement rate explains why residents are still stepping in the stuff — was hard to argue with. Int 0864-2026, requiring a two-year composting pilot for dog waste collected at dog runs, was the bill Parks most clearly opposed, calling it outside the agency's mission and resource-intensive given that Parks employees do not currently enter dog runs for routine cleaning.

Int 0859-2026 (CM Brewer) was the least contested item. It provides a legislative pathway for DORIS to post independently researched biographical information about honorees when none exists in the Council's legislative record — primarily addressing the 9/11 first responder street namings from 2002 and 2003 where signs went up faster than biographies could be compiled. New DORIS Commissioner Sean Smith (one month in the job) testified that the agency has already built an interactive map of over 4,200 street co-namings dating back to 1902, researched 393 biographies connected to the 9/11 namings, and is down to one unidentified honoree on Staten Island. Parks noted its own practices already largely align with the bill's intent and expressed willingness to work with DORIS on implementation. CM Morano used the DORIS testimony to make a pointed broader argument: if a 50-person agency can build a map of 4,200 historical records with full biographies, larger agencies cannot credibly claim it is too hard to publish data they already hold.
