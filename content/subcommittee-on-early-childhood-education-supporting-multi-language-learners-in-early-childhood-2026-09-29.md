Summary

Meeting Overview

The Subcommittee on Early Childhood Education, chaired by Jennifer Gutierrez, held an oversight hearing on two items: T2026-2404, examining how the City supports multilingual learners in early childhood education, and T2026-2581 (Res 0650-2026), a resolution opposing the federal proposed rule titled "Reducing Federal Burden for Head Start Programs," which would among other things require all Head Start instruction to be conducted solely in English. The hearing came against the backdrop of the Trump administration's executive order designating English as the official language of the United States and a notice of proposed rulemaking on English instruction requirements in Head Start programs, with a public comment deadline of October 6.

The Mamdani administration sent Emmy List from the Mayor's Office of Child Care and Early Childhood Education and Eric Phillips, the newly appointed Deputy Chancellor for Early Childhood Education at NYC Public Schools, along with several senior officials. Their testimony was thorough and generally positive in tone, describing a system with 99 verified dual language bilingual education sites across the city, covering Spanish, Mandarin, Russian, French, Italian, Polish, Korean and Arabic. They described the identification process for emergent multilingual learners in early childhood, which relies on family self-reporting via the state's Emergent Multilingual Learner Language Profile rather than the formal NYSITELL assessment used in K-12. The administration emphasized culturally and linguistically responsive instruction across all programs, job-embedded coaching for educators, and the aspiration to expand dual language offerings into 3-K, noting two newly opened 3-K dual language programs this year in Districts 15 and 31. They were notably candid that there are no additional state mandates requiring multilingual supports in early childhood settings -- the Division of Early Childhood Education has made this a policy commitment of its own rather than a legal requirement. On funding, the administration confirmed there is no dedicated funding stream specifically for dual language pre-K programs beyond per-seat allocations, with a supplemental bilingual revitalization school allocation memorandum distributing around $1.3 million to 193 schools for professional learning, family engagement and supplemental materials.

The Independent Budget Office, represented by Emma Gossett, provided useful quantitative context, noting that 34% of pre-K students enrolled in NYC Public Schools operated or contracted programs spoke a language other than English at home, across more than 140 languages, and that 73% of New York City's 18,000 Head Start students in 2024-2025 were dual language learners who speak Spanish at home. The IBO flagged that if the proposed federal rule takes effect, families currently served by Head Start's multilingual programming will look to NYC Public Schools and private providers to fill the gap -- a system that, as the hearing made clear, is already under-resourced relative to demand. Applications for dual language pre-K slots showed 1,574 for Spanish alone, dwarfing available supply.

Community advocates and service providers added important texture. Advocates for Children flagged a serious compliance failure: of roughly 4,000 preschoolers entitled to bilingual special education programs and services in 2024-2025, 65% did not receive them -- a legal obligation, not a policy aspiration. Joanne Derwin of One World Project, the Spanish immersion CBO in Windsor Terrace that Gutierrez had visited, described running a universal pre-K program at a $120,000 annual deficit because the DOE per-seat rate does not cover costs, and noted she had turned down a 3-K contract for the same reason. Early Starters described working with newly arrived families in shelters who face not only language barriers but fears about sharing basic information like zip codes with city systems. City's First Readers and Literacy in Community Link emphasized the importance of home language as the foundation for literacy and called for investment in family-centered early literacy programs and educator professional development. The hearing generated real energy around expansion of dual language into 3-K and 2-K and better alignment between community-based providers and the public system, but no formal votes were taken and no legislation was passed.

Numbers

- Approximately 45% of children ages zero to five in New York State identify as multilingual learners, above the national average of 32%.
- NYC Public Schools currently operates 99 verified dual language bilingual education program sites.
- Total emergent multilingual learners across the early childhood portfolio (pre-K, 3-K, 2-K and infant programs) as of current enrollment: 30,058.
- Breakdown: 12,725 in pre-K; 6,737 in 3-K; 581 in 2-K; 15 in infant programs.
- 34% of pre-K students enrolled in NYC Public Schools operated or contracted programs spoke a language other than English at home.
- Families served across 3-K and pre-K programs speak over 140 different languages.
- The five most common home languages across 3-K and pre-K: Spanish (18% of all enrolled students), followed by Chinese languages, Bengali, Russian and Arabic.
- Pre-K dual language programs received the following applications by language: 1,574 Spanish, 129 Chinese, 100 French, 60 Russian, 47 Italian, 24 Korean, 17 Arabic, 3 Polish.
- 3-K dual language programs received 60 applications for Spanish and 28 for Russian.
- Over 80% of families were able to get one of their top school choices for dual language programs.
- Dual language programs target a 50/50 split between native speakers of the partner language and English-speaking families opting in.
- 108 teachers are certified as lead teachers in dual language classrooms; 183 pre-K dual language licensed teachers hold a birth-to-two certificate or are on a pathway to it.
- New York State requires 15% of all continuing teacher and leader professional learning to be focused on multilingual learners; bilingual-certified teachers must devote 50% of their professional learning to multilingual learners.
- Funding for programs offering dual language learning in early childhood is approximately $30 million.
- A bilingual revitalization school allocation memorandum distributed approximately $1.3 million to 193 schools for professional learning, family engagement and supplemental materials; new programs received $1,000 each.
- NYC Head Start programs in 2024-2025 served 18,000 children across 150 programs, including 900 homeless students, over 600 students with IEPs, approximately 30 foster children and over 580 pregnant women.
- 73% of New York City's enrolled Head Start students -- approximately 14,000 children -- were dual language learners who speak Spanish at home.
- About 25% of Head Start staff spoke Spanish; 35% of staff were proficient in a language other than English; over 400 families participated in ESL training over the course of the year.
- Over 800 parent volunteers participated in Head Start programs in 2024-2025.
- Of approximately 4,000 preschoolers entitled to bilingual special education programs and services in 2024-2025, 65% did not receive them.
- One World Project's universal pre-K contract generates an annual deficit of approximately $120,000; the DOE rate leaves teachers earning roughly $25,000 less per year than their public school counterparts.
- NYC Public Schools previously served as grant recipient for approximately 30% of Head Start programs in the city; their grant was not renewed and they no longer oversee Head Start programs.

Action Points

- NYC Public Schools to provide the committee with the locations of the two new 3-K dual language programs (confirmed as District 15 for Spanish and District 31 for Russian, but formal follow-up on full details requested).
- NYC Public Schools to provide the committee with data on how many existing kindergarten dual language programs also have a pre-K dual language component.
- NYC Public Schools to provide the committee with annual data on how many dual language programs have been closed in recent years.
- NYC Public Schools officials to visit One World Project in Windsor Terrace, as encouraged by Chair Gutierrez.
- NYC Public Schools to follow up with Advocates for Children regarding a specific family waiting for a special education evaluation in Bengali to determine current status and expedite resolution.
- Administration to improve information in MySchools directories so families can clearly understand what enhanced language supports are available at each program and what they entail, per advocacy community recommendation.
- Administration to conduct more robust and targeted multilingual outreach to immigrant communities about Two-K, Three-K and Pre-K programs and provide hands-on support through the application and registration process.
- Administration to examine data on which kindergarten dual language programs lack a corresponding pre-K dual language program and develop a plan to address continuity gaps.
- Administration to accelerate the classroom conversion strategy to open new dual language classrooms in schools already operating dual language programs.
- Administration to develop and disseminate clearer, family-facing communications about dual language and multilingual programming options to improve public awareness, as urged by CM Brewer.
- NYC Public Schools to share data with the committee on progress of the bilingual educator pipeline and pathways for educators to obtain bilingual extensions.
- Chair Gutierrez to encourage members of the public to submit comments to the Federal Register on the proposed Head Start rule changes before the October 6 deadline.
- Administration to follow up with the committee on how schools are supported when considering opening a new pre-K dual language program within an existing kindergarten dual language school.
- Early Starters to continue workforce development collaboration with CUNY and Bank Street College of Education and to pursue expanded apprenticeship slots beyond the current five.
- City's First Readers to continue outreach to nontraditional settings including clinics, hospitals and WIC centers, and to pursue technology investment to allow smaller partners to update the program directory without manual intervention.
