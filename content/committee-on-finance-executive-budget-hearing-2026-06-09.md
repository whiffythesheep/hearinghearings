Summary

Meeting Overview

The Finance Committee held the third segment of its FY2027 Executive Budget hearings, taking testimony from the Department of Finance (DOF) and the Independent Budget Office (IBO). The hearing covered a wide range of fiscal and operational matters, with property tax reform, debt collection, the pied-a-terre surcharge, deed theft prevention and Fair Fares expansion receiving the most substantive attention.

Commissioner Richard Lee, appearing for the first time before the Council in his new role, gave an overview of DOF priorities: onboarding 50 new auditors (projected to yield an additional $50 million in audit revenue in FY27), recovering $9 million from Amazon for idling violations, and initiating a 30-day internal review of debt collection practices. He also flagged a ten-year miscalculation in Section 1127 returns, in which City employees living outside the five boroughs were incorrectly receiving a STAR credit on both state and City sides simultaneously, costing roughly $10-12 million per year. The error has been corrected for tax year 2025 only, with no clawback for prior years.

Property tax reform generated the most pointed exchange. Chair Lee and Deputy Speaker Williams pressed the administration on when a formal proposal would be submitted to Albany. The Commissioner acknowledged that substantive reform requires state legislative action, and suggested the next state legislative session as the target window. When pressed on whether the administration had made any formal submission to the state, the answer was essentially no, with the explanation that closing a $12 billion budget gap had consumed the bandwidth. The First Deputy Commissioner added useful technical context: the assessment cap structure disproportionately burdens neighborhoods that have not gentrified, because caps prevent assessed values from keeping pace with actual market values in gentrifying areas while holding effective tax rates high elsewhere. This is the core structural inequity that reform must address, and it requires state action on the cap mechanism. The lien sale pause and land bank creation were discussed; the six-month review of lien enforcement ends this summer, and nominees for the land bank working group are due July 28.

IBO Director Louisa gave testimony covering a range of fiscal risks. She flagged a childcare voucher funding cliff in which City funding drops to $818 million annually from 2028, creating a potential shortfall approaching $1 billion per year, with 25,000 children already on a closed waitlist. She noted that Tier 6 pension enhancements will cost $41 million more than budgeted in FY27, rising to $69 million by 2030. On the pied-a-terre surcharge, IBO declined to validate the $500 million revenue estimate, citing uncertainty around property valuation methodology, administration and behavioral responses. IBO also raised significant concern about the federal policy environment: 43,500 New Yorkers face near-term loss of SNAP benefits, and up to 225,000 New York City residents will lose health insurance as the federal changes undercut the State Essential Plan, with neither the state nor the City having budgeted for this. The discussion of Fair Fares produced a genuinely useful policy suggestion from Chair Lee: using Department of Finance income data, modeled on how the Earned Income Tax Credit is administered, to automatically notify eligible residents rather than relying on them to self-enroll.

- DOF is onboarding 50 new auditors, projected to increase audit revenue by $50 million in FY27.
- Section 1127 miscalculation cost approximately $10-12 million per year over roughly ten years, for a total estimated lost revenue exceeding $100 million.
- Approximately 4,000 City employees are currently subject to the Section 1127 waiver.
- DOF identified approximately 304 business tax refunds potentially issuable by end of FY26, totaling approximately $81 million.
- Co-op and condo abatement compliance mailings are projected to yield $13 million per year in additional revenue.
- The pied-a-terre surcharge is projected to raise $500 million annually and affect approximately 8,000 properties.
- Class 1 properties valued at $5-15 million will face a surcharge rate of approximately 1.05 percent; those at $25 million and above face 1.3 percent.
- Class A office parcels declined roughly 4 percent on the FY27 final roll, representing a loss of more than 7 million square feet and a 15 percent decrease in taxable assessed value for that class.
- Rent regulated buildings had their market value reduced by 3.3 percent on average and taxes reduced by 1.3 percent following a 50 basis point increase in the capitalization rate on the FY27 assessment roll.
- Approximately 110,000 children currently receive childcare vouchers; over 25,000 are on the waitlist with the portal closed since 2025.
- IBO estimates the City needs an additional $78 million in FY27, rising to approximately $1 billion annually in FY28, to address the childcare funding cliff.
- Tier 6 pension enhancements will require $41 million above the Executive Budget in FY27, growing to $69 million by FY30.
- Approximately 43,500 New Yorkers face near-term loss of SNAP benefits due to federal changes.
- Up to 450,000 people statewide, approximately half in New York City, will lose health insurance as federal changes undercut the State Essential Plan.
- The Executive Budget increases funding for power and energy by $376 million in FY27, rising to $477 million by FY30, bringing total citywide building energy costs to $1.3 billion.
- City FHEPS is budgeted at $2 billion, with a plan to achieve $235 million in unspecified administrative streamlining annually.
- State housing voucher program will yield approximately $33 million for the City, supporting approximately 9,100 vouchers.
- The Sheriff's Office has a full authorized headcount of 320, with 260 active and approximately 50-60 vacancies.
- NYCHA capital subsidies from the City were approximately $1.2 billion in FY26, $136 million in FY27, and $210 million in FY28.
